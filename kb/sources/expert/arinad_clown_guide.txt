Introduction
Hello there! In this guide, I am going to dissect and explain using intricate details how one is supposed to use Clown’s power at the highest level. Many people who play Clown are led to believe either through their own experiences and/or the experiences of others that Clown’s yellow bottles are either not useful or only capable of limited returns. Using video examples, picture blueprints, and textual analysis, this guide aims to dispel any of those beliefs as well as impart new strategies on how to use them.
Based on the conclusions of my findings, mastery of Clown’s yellow bottle is the pinnacle of understanding Clown’s maximum capability. When Clown’s yellow bottles are used properly alongside his pink bottles, it enables so many more instances where safe situations very quickly become lethal. Knowing how to correctly use your bottles together at a loop is only part of it (even around a pre-dropped pallet). At the highest level of gameplay where survivors like to chain together multiple tiles, you need to know how to combine your bottles without stopping your movement while also adapting to sudden changes in direction.
All of the information in this guide is NOT meant to be read in one sitting. Further, only the first 32 pages are strictly focused on teaching new fundamental information regarding strategy. Afterward, the guide transitions to actual examples by taking a closer look at specific maps, talking about their general characteristics, and then looking at the technique I employed across their different tiles. The last chapter of the guide is a repository for extra tips and discussion regarding matters like perk choice, build design, and my Add-on Tier List. The montage directory and FAQ are also located under here, etc.
I recommend reading the first 37 pages to get an understanding of the fundamentals and from there you should be able to flip through the rest of the guide as you please. Make sure you watch every video during the fundamental chapters, as well. Do NOT skip the videos on How to Read a Chase Blueprint (35 mins) OR Double-Bottle Chase Analysis (2 hours). I know they are long, but that is only because I went through the effort to explicitly cover as many bases as possible.
Lastly, I want it to be known in case it is not clear that I do not consider myself the sole dominant authority for any opinion or statement of fact that I make throughout this guide. However, I have been a Clown-only main for the last year and a half. Throughout that time, I have only improved my understanding of Clown’s power as a result of my experimenting with my own gameplay. I have not picked up any new methods or strategy from watching other Clown players. In turn, I would say that I qualify as an authority based on the origin of my playstyle. Regardless, I still prefer to let my results speak for themselves. The purpose of writing this guide is so that I can help others play Clown better – not to be used as a throne.

These conceptual terms were molded as a result of my own gameplay as I devised new strategies in chase. In turn, the names/ways that I describe these concepts are based on my own learned understanding. Pictures will be displayed alongside the terms to help make the text descriptions clearer. Make sure you learn these terms before you start looking at actual chase examples otherwise the chase blueprints won’t make much sense. Important Terms
Course of Action - A string of tactics executed in sequence in order to hit a survivor at a pallet tile. This can include anything from faking a pallet kick, when you choose to throw what bottle, where you choose to throw what bottle, and more. Think of it like solving a puzzle. A Course of Action is essentially what is being done across the steps of a Chase Blueprint. Further details are explained under the section Learning How to Double-Bottle Combo (page 16).
Pallet Possession - The state of control over an undropped pallet at a loop. If a survivor is closer to the pallet than the killer within a short radius, then that survivor has possession of the pallet (and vice versa). The term “possession” comes from the fact that whoever has it possesses control over what is done with the pallet . If the killer has possession of the pallet, the survivor is not going to be able to reach the pallet without getting hit. Similarly, a survivor in possession of the pallet is ‘safe’ from the killer since they can either pre-drop the pallet or potentially hit the killer with a pallet stun.
Conceding the Pallet - When a survivor currently in possession of a pallet voluntarily relinquishes that possession to the killer. Using the above diagram as an example, the top half shows a survivor in possession of a pallet at an I-Tile. At that moment, the survivor is currently safe from the killer since they can either pre-drop the pallet or wait/attempt a pallet stun. However, what if they decide to run the loop for another lap because they have extra distance? By opting for another lap, that survivor concedes possession of the pallet to the killer.
As shown in the bottom half of the diagram, running away from the pallet as the survivor means that you’re outside the area of possession and, subsequently, the killer is inside of it. Unlike other killers, Clown is able to take advantage of survivors who concede possession of the pallet . Using his power, Clown can combine both his yellow and pink bottles together in a way where it becomes impossible for survivors to retake possession of the pallet before they get hit.

I started off this chase with a very quick injury at a nearby pallet loop. 1. Jeryl used the speed boost to run toward the Killer Shack with me right behind him. 2. I noticed as I was entering the shack that he purposefully chose not to drop the pallet when he could have used it to buy himself extra time. This is known as conceding the pallet . He was able to reach the pallet before I could stop him and yet he chose to give up that possession. I then threw a yellow bottle on the doorway as I passed through it and walked halfway through the shack in order to try and push him further along the right-side wall. 3. Afterward, I doubled-back through my yellow cloud and circled around the right hoping to ambush him. What I didn’t realize was that he didn’t run along the right-side wall and instead he went along the left. Knowing now that he went along the left wall, 4. I threw a pink bottle near the window on the right in the event that he was further up alongside the left wall and chose to commit outside the shack. I then ran around the shack from the left side and was able to confirm the hit before he made it to the pallet. This was back when survivors could use Dead Hard for distance, as well. The loop has become a lot more unsafe after the Dead Hard rework since it has become harder for survivors to get close enough to the pallet in time.
Many survivors tend to concede possession of the Killer Shack pallet to Clown fairly easily (likely due to a lack of experience against better Clowns). All it takes is a little distance between Clown and the survivor and you can almost guarantee that they will concede the pallet. With the proper distance and efficient movement on the survivor’s part, it’s very unlikely that you will catch them with pink bottles alone . In turn, you’re going to waste time both running the loop and then afterward kicking the pallet.
While some people prefer to get the shack pallet out of the way, it’s unnecessary (assuming the survivor chooses not to drop it) with the inclusion of yellow bottles to make a double bottle combo. Being able to hit survivors before they make it back to the pallet means that your overall lethality should also increase if you know they are going to stay at the shack.  Further, the mere existence of an undropped shack pallet can serve as bait to more survivors that are unaware of your capability. It’s not uncommon to end 3 chases or more at Killer Shack with the pallet remaining undropped as long as you play it properly since survivors tend to be reluctant to drop it.
The first thing you should do is establish what side of the shack has the pallet. You never want to chase a survivor through the open doorway into the shack toward the shack pallet unless there is so much distance that you know they aren’t going to drop the pallet. If the open doorway is what’s closest, you should typically circle around the side of the shack to the doorway with the pallet. The survivor might beat you there first, but if you give them just a tiny bit of distance they’re more likely to concede the pallet and let you set up your bottles. If they decide to camp and then drop the pallet, they lose the opportunity to create early distance. You can then just kick the pallet and Killer Shack becomes a free tile for the rest of the match.

Slicing the Tile - When a survivor commits to a tile or runs in a way where either clever positioning and/or bottle placement on your part can cut them off from the rest of that tile or certain areas of the map. You want to constantly shift your movement in both directions so that they don’t know what direction to go. It is mainly done so that you can either reload without them gaining distance or hit them easily with a double-bottle combo. Some survivors may try to leave early. This allows you to cancel your reload and catch up to them before they can reach a safe area. Tile-slicing can take place virtually anywhere on a map (such as pallet loops or jungle gyms) but it is most prominent at tiles near the far edges or inside enclosed rooms.
Typically for jungle gyms , this is in the form of a really long wall with a singular window or a pallet spawn. As Clown, your bottles when used together let you slice these tiles in half. Instead of being a jungle gym with multiple walls and turns, it’s merely a long wall with either a window or a pallet. Further, the combination of both yellow and pink bottles makes both the window or pallet spawn unsafe if the survivor attempts to vault them.
With pallet loops specifically, tile slicing is mostly effective at tiles that are either inside rooms or positioned closer to edges of the map. In these circumstances, survivors are essentially ‘trapped’ at the tile due to the surrounding walls restricting their movement. While there may be multiple doorways to abandon the tile, Clown is able to position both himself and his bottles in a way that forces survivors to run the loop. More on this will be explained below in the video How to Read a Chase Blueprint (page 11) and also within the section Location-Based Pallet Techniques and a Closer Look at Pallet Possession (page 18) .
Both of these rough sketches above are meant to illustrate two different types of jungle gyms that might typically be frustrating for the average M1 killer. Clown, however, is able to slice survivors away from the whole other half of the tile by combining his yellow and pink bottles. Depending on where the jungle gym is located on the map, this can either force them into the pink or into a dead zone away from the tile where they would still otherwise be more vulnerable. Assuming the survivor runs into the pink, they will not be able to make it to either the window or the pallet before you catch them with your invigoration buff.
You might have noticed that there is no area of possession in either examples. That is because in both circumstances we’re assuming the pallet has already been dropped and has not been kicked. Looking at the example on the right, the bottom jungle gym wall under the pallet is very short. This enables you to set up your yellow in a way where you can either hit them at the pallet vault if they try to 50/50, or you can combine it with a pink at the top corner and loop them around before they make it back to the pallet.
Momentum - Put simply, this is the speed of your execution. However, its true significance translates into much more than that. Every bit of success from your bottle placement to your physical movement is defined by the intensity of your momentum . As you become more and more capable of acting faster and moving with calculated purpose, new possibilities for success using your bottles start to present themselves to you in spontaneous fashion. The lower your momentum, the more you’re going to find yourself restricted when it comes to using your bottles in order to orchestrate hits. It can even potentially be the difference in whether or not a hit becomes implausible and you have to kick a pallet.
Improving one’s momentum is not something that can be directly instilled through words or even video explanation. In order to achieve higher momentum, one must dedicate themselves to continually practicing and practicing Clown by their own volition . As you continually practice the strategies I have described within this guide, you will slowly begin to notice that it becomes easier for you to set up your bottle combinations faster and, as a result, your chase times keep getting shorter. You may experience some hardship when you make mistakes, but that is normal with self-improvement when there is still much to learn.
Using myself as an example, momentum is an area of Clown expertise that even I haven’t fully mastered yet. Day after day, I’m still finding myself getting better and better at moving faster and acting smarter. In a lot of ways, this has led to me viewing momentum as like the glass ceiling of playing Clown at the highest level. As I improve, I continue to shatter that glass ceiling and move on to the next layer that’s above it. This same logic also applies to each and every one of you. Regardless of whatever difficulty you may currently face in execution, just know that with time you will eventually overcome that difficulty and achieve much greater results.
I’ll go ahead and be the first one to say here that I’ve made my fair share of mistakes over the course of some of my matches in the pursuit of trying to keep high momentum by playing around a pre-dropped pallet. I’m proud to own up to that. Despite any humility I have had to bear in the face of my failures, I can say with objective confidence that I have passively learned from those mistakes as well as conditioned myself to perform better in those situations time after time again. That is the essence of practice makes perfect .
Although I cannot use video examples or explanations in order to actually teach any of you how to improve your momentum without hands-on practice, I feel like there might be value to be gained from breaking down in a video some examples of what high momentum looks like in a chase when it was absolutely necessary. Again, this video won’t teach you how to get high momentum, but it will show you what high momentum looks like when you are capable of playing Clown at a really high level.

How to Read a Chase Blueprint
Why the Yellow?
Clown’s yellow bottles open up a level of strategy that would otherwise be impossible using only pinks. For a good handful of maps at the majority of tiles, if a survivor is efficient with their movement and you’re only using pinks, you’re very unlikely to reach them before they make it back to a window. This fact has led to the prevailing sentiment of counterplay against Clown to be merely pre-dropping pallets. Since most Clowns do not use the yellow bottles, not a lot of survivors have experience against it. You cannot counter a Clown that uses his yellow and pink bottles together by simply pre-dropping the pallet. If anything, you are actually making the chase easier for Clown to some degree by removing the threat of a pallet stun. Yellow and pink bottles used together enable Clown to catch up to survivors even before they can make it back to a pre-dropped pallet. As Clown, this increases your lethality by creating opportunities for faster hits and reducing how long you artificially extend your chases by kicking pallets.
A Clown that doesn’t use his yellows in chase and also has to kick plenty of pallets is going to waste a lot of time during the most crucial stage of the game without getting any downs or hook pressure – the early game. Survivors are at their strongest in the early-game , and you need your first down to come quick. They will always be at their maximum pallet count at the very beginning of every match. If you spend the early stages kicking pallets, you’re giving them absolutely every advantage during the early-game period to snowball their control to a point where you can’t recover. Plenty of maps are either large enough in size or have so many pallets that you just can’t afford to kick them unless absolutely necessary
Opting not to break pallets puts survivors in an extremely awkward position. This gets them thinking about how choosing to drop the pallet forces them to lose distance that they would have otherwise gained by running forward. However, not dropping the pallet and running forward also has the possibility of leaving them vulnerable before they make it to another tile. If the Clown is using both his yellows and pinks together, it becomes even harder to gauge if that would be the outcome. Survivors are thus forced to think on their feet in a way where they don’t actually have confidence that they’re making the right decision.
I’ve gone against players where after hitting a survivor using a complex double-bottle setup they lose all courage at every pallet loop for the rest of the match. They go from willingly running unsafe loops to abandoning much stronger loops at the drop of a yellow bottle. It is because now they respect what it is they have seen I’m capable of doing and still don’t know what else to expect going forward. The only fact they know for certain is that I am looking for any chance they are willing to give me in order to hit them. Their whole behavior changes to where the way they play chases is strictly about creating as much distance as possible instead of efficiently trying to stall for time by looping at pallets.

Learning How to Double-Bottle Combo
Before one can learn how to use both their yellow and pink bottles together, it’s important to understand that both bottles serve two different functions to achieve the same end. Clown’s pink bottles are immediate, stronger in effect, and shorter in duration . His yellow bottles are delayed, weaker in effect, and significantly longer in duration . Not all circumstances call for yellows and not all circumstances call for pinks. Some circumstances call for both. It’s all about making sure you use the right tool(s) for the right job.
Identifying when situations call for yellow bottles comes from plotting a course of action before you execute it. Refer to the definition under Important Terms and the video above titled “ How to Read a Chase Blueprint ” for more context if needed. As the person throwing the bottles, you possess the prior knowledge of what you attempt to do with them. Survivors can only guess. Even then, there’s very little room for counterplay when both bottles are optimally combined together. The speed difference between Clown and the survivor becomes a colossal 40-44% (with and without add-ons). Not even Dead Hard for distance back when it was still around was strong enough to counter yellows + pinks at a variety of loops (Killer Shack being one of them). In order to understand how to plan a proper course of action , we should take a look at the different roles both yellow and pink bottles serve.
The role of the yellow bottle is the initiator . It should almost always be the first bottle that you throw when planning a double-bottle combo. So much so, that you might as well consider this a rule of thumb. Although the effect comes out on a delay, that doesn’t mean you’re limited in continuing your course of action until it is active. You should be factoring that delay into your course of action and supplementing it with the necessary tactics. These tactics can be anything from moving/facing another side of the loop to shove them further inside, pretending to break a pallet, pretending to vault a window, faking a reload while still having available bottles, throwing a pink bottle in conjunction with the yellow, and more. A very generalized way to understand how to use yellow bottles in practice is: 1. Throw Yellow Bottle 2. ???? (Pick one or more if necessary from the above) 3. Profit (Run through the yellow). When it comes to specific examples for what to do in chase, those will be covered very explicitly in the bottom half. I encourage you to defer to this above generalization when viewing these specific examples as that should hopefully make the concept more clear.
As for Clown’s pink bottles, their purpose is best served as the finisher. What makes pink bottles excellent finishers is that they inhibit fast vaulting at windows while also being extremely hard to play around due to their effect coming out immediately. At the same time, although the slowdown is more powerful and comes out immediately, the shorter duration means that you will close less distance between yourself and the survivor using just a pink bottle as opposed to using a yellow bottle instead. The optimal threshold for only using a pink bottle is if lethality comes within 2.5 seconds of the slowdown. Anything more than that, and you’re likely to need a second bottle. Once two bottles become necessary, the justification for initially using a yellow first instead of a pink becomes significantly more valid. The hindrance from your pink bottles cannot be compounded by additional pink bottles. However, you can absolutely compound your yellow’s haste with your pink’s hindrance if you can correctly anticipate that the next hit in the chase is going to last long enough to require two bottles.
Location-Based Pallet Techniques and a Closer Look at Pallet Possession
A large factor in how you plan and execute your course of action at a pallet tile is going to be dependent on the location. Many different maps replicate the same individual tile. However, the proximity of other nearby tiles is more often than not different across each map. Sometimes, you might have a jungle gym next to the Killer Shack. Other times, it might be something specific like a school bus or a giant crane on Autohaven. Due to these outcomes, it’s important to reposition the survivor (through your movement) and yourself in a way where they can’t take advantage of any nearby tiles when you set up your double-bottle combos. If you have planned your movement correctly and a survivor attempts abandoning a tile after you have thrown a yellow bottle, it’s possible in a good amount of situations to rotate through that yellow bottle for speed, slow them down with a pink, and catch them before they make it to another tile.
These types of situations are also where the importance of pallet possession really comes into play. If a Clown isn’t going to kick a pallet regardless if it gets dropped or not, then that means pre-dropping the pallet doesn’t alleviate much pressure (if any pressure at all) for the survivor. If we also assume that the survivor is cut off by the Clown from any other nearby tiles, then that means the only saving grace the survivor has is succeeding a pallet stun. When both the survivor and Clown are aware of these facts, it turns the area of possession around an undropped pallet into a constant tug of war . The survivor is aware that he has only one chance at succeeding a pallet stun and using that time to create distance. Similarly, the Clown is aware that he has to respect the potential of a pallet stun while also being positioned to take advantage of the pallet being dropped early.
As the Clown player in these situations, it’s usually not worth it to take risks on getting a hit by swinging through a pallet. If that risk feels present, it’s usually better to reset your positioning to lock them at the tile, reload your bottles if necessary, and adapt by doing a different combo around it. Eating a pallet stun is a guaranteed loss of pressure that becomes even worse if you also happen to be out of bottles for the follow up. Choosing to reset when they’re locked down means they don’t have any opportunity to create distance off of you reloading and you can immediately attempt a different combo right after.

Not only was his movement extremely efficient, but he was also cognizant of the fact that I was purposefully playing around him pre-dropping the pallet as well as trying to avoid a pallet stun. It was a chase that was quite unlike many others I’d had before, as it was one of the few times up to that point where I had been challenged to improvise a new double-bottle combo in the heat of the moment without slowing my momentum.
Slowing down your momentum in chase around a pallet allows the survivor to mentally re-focus their attention as well as creates new opportunities for them to escape a tile. I had to ensure that I kept myself constantly moving to keep him second guessing on his feet as I came up with a new combo. Given that I knew he wasn’t prone to pre-drop the pallet, that meant I also had to combo my bottles together in a way where I didn’t push him directly through the pallet’s area of possession . Kicking Pallets – When is it Necessary, and When is it Harmful?
With how powerful your bottles are when combined together, kicking pallets should be seen as a last resort. It takes time to kick a pallet and that time has consequences. The worst time to ever kick a pallet is when you’re in the middle of a chase. Survivors that are efficient with their movement and cognizant of their location are capable of using the time you spend kicking a pallet to create distance and stall you even further (especially if they have Windows of Opportunity). Even after downing a survivor , choosing to kick a pallet before hooking that survivor can circumstantially be the difference in applying enough generator regression to stop a generator from being completed.
Time is very important when you play a killer like Clown that lacks map mobility. If you spend a lot of time in chase without getting a hook, you haven’t created any lasting pressure toward actually winning the game. While injuries may seem like lasting pressure, this only ends up being the reality if you can convert that pre-injury into a quick hook later on in another chase. Assuming that a survivor heals themselves afterward (either using a medkit or another survivor), the time you spent in that previous chase breaking those pallets becomes worth either a zero or negative sum. Most maps have enough pallets to negate the downside of creating early dead zones. If the early dead zones you end up creating are also around an area of the map with completed generators, that time wasted kicking pallets creates even less value.
The only circumstances where kicking a pallet in or out of chase is completely necessary is when that dropped pallet obstructs a pathway or creates an unstoppable loop. In these situations, you quite literally have no other alternative. Thankfully, these pallets are typically few and far in between. Unless you’re on Gideon Meat Plant or a few other select maps, it’s unlikely you should need to break more than three pallets a match. If you’re using Cigar Box , you should be able to get away without kicking a pre-dropped pallet even at jungle gyms depending on the layout and your bottle setup.
It should also be mentioned that dropped pallets in chase can actually be disadvantageous for survivors. Vaulting a pallet is a specific action that is always executed in the exact same way. It is not a form of movement that provides survivors with free control as they do it. While vaulting a pallet, a survivor is vulnerable to being hit during the time they are sliding across the pallet and shortly afterward. This is because when a survivor vaults a pallet, they are stuck in what is known as an animation lock . An animation lock is when a character is scripted to undergo a specific action without any user input for control. Kicking pallets, picking up survivors, breaking walls, opening lockers, and vaulting windows are all examples of animation locks. When vaulting a pallet, a survivor is stuck in an animation lock that prevents them from re-vaulting a pallet for a short period of time afterward.
Let’s Talk About Survivor Efficiency and the Necessity of Tunneling
While Clown might excel very well in 1v1 situations, he struggles significantly against coordinated teams that understand the source of his weaknesses. Specifically, this is Clown’s poor map mobility. In order for Clown to do anything at all, you have to physically roam the map at base movement speed. Using your bottles for mobility is only to be done seldomly or for the utility from Cigar Box since a lower bottle count can set you behind during the following chase. If you get a map like Mother’s Dwelling, Ormond, Eyrie of Crows, or Temple of Purgation (plus more), it becomes physically impossible for you to simultaneously pressure the hooked survivor and protect your generators on the other side of the map. Smart survivors who know you are not within the general area of a hooked survivor will purposefully let that survivor sit on a hook for most of a single hook state in order to get more generator progress. This is especially true following the removal of hook grabs, the implementation of the anti-camp meter, and the extension of hook state timers by 10 seconds. They will go for the unhook at the very last second before the hooked survivor hits the next hook state in order to maximize their repair progress.
Against this type of strategy, there is very little you can do to threaten the team by focusing everyone equally. You need to consolidate your focus exclusively on the survivors you have closest to being sacrificed. Taking a single survivor out of the match through relentless pressure is the best chance you have at slowing down generator speeds enough so that you can win the match. It enables the proceeding hooks to become more threatening in addition to enabling greater snowball potential. It puts the impetus on the remaining survivors to be even more efficient in order to make up for being down a teammate. This can result in survivors either taking dangerous risks or making stressful mistakes to compensate for their lost efficiency.
However, this isn’t the only type of scenario where you will find tunneling to be absolutely necessary. Even outside of large maps, generators can be finished very quickly if the team knows how to do them and the survivor you initially chase also knows how to use their resources. Remember, survivors at the start of every match have every pallet available to them. If the survivor you find first doesn’t make many mistakes in chase and plays safely, it may still take you anywhere from 50-60 seconds to take them down. That is enough time for two survivors to finish one generator and for a third survivor to have another generator at 50% progress. This means that at the start of the match, you may end up with 3.5 generators remaining following your first chase without factoring in any generator perks, great skill checks, or toolboxes . At this type of pace, if you spread your hooks, your generators will be completed by the time you have 5 hooks if not sooner. This is also just the baseline of situations. It is important for you to take into account that a lot of survivors like to run Medkits, a lot of survivors like to ‘pre-run’ (preemptively create distance) before the chase actually begins, and MANY will have Dead Hard . When either of these factors come into play, the dynamics of survivor efficiency drastically increases even further.
Looking first at Medkits and toolboxes, Medkits are arguably stronger than toolboxes for how versatile they are in addition to being more consistent. Unless a survivor gets caught out while healing or the killer has hemorrhage and the heal gets interrupted, they are always going to be getting full value out of their medkit charges. Hemorrhage is pretty uncommon of a debuff and only available from a very small number of perks. Clown does not have any add-ons that apply hemorrhage – just Mangled. Mangled only reduces the speed that they heal; it does not require them to use more charges. Toolboxes, on the other-hand, can be counteracted by the many generator perks that you are likely to be running. While a toolbox enables you to complete generators within clutch timeframes, the time window to complete a generator without a toolbox is already fast enough. Outside of those clutch situations, this means that all toolboxes really accomplish is ending the game faster in a match where you, as Clown, were destined to lose to begin with.
Medkits, on the other hand, literally create lose/lose situations for you as Clown if you aren’t able to quickly down a survivor. When a survivor has a Medkit, you can’t afford to leave a chase early. You don’t know what kind of Medkit they have or how many times they can heal themselves. If you decide to leave, that survivor is going to spend less time healing than you spent chasing them and in turn will exponentially convert the time you spent chasing them into a win/win for their team. As Clown , you are also likely still going to lose those generators due to your poor map mobility making it impossible to contest them before they are completed. To make matters worse, now everybody is also healthy and you have zero hooks for your time.
Pre-running is probably one of Clown’s greatest counters and a strategy you will see exhibited by many skilled survivors. It’s very simple: when you know Clown is coming, you leave before he actually gets there. As an M1 killer, Clown can’t effectively deal with pre-running without using his bottles early in order to merely close the distance. However, doing so means that Clown will have less bottles available for when the chase itself actually begins. This pretty much ensures that Clown will have to reload mid-chase and, as a result, undo all pressure gained from using those bottles earlier to begin with. On certain indoor maps like Lery’s Memorial or Racoon Police Department, consistent pre-running will deny you from using your bottles in any meaningful way and artificially extend most chase times to where they are borderline unviable.
Lastly, and probably the worst of them all, is Dead Hard . The sheer flexibility, lack of downsides or restrictive conditions, its elusive nature (you never know a survivor has it until you either see them use it or another exhaustion perk), and the potential value makes Dead Hard ubiquitously the strongest exhaustion perk in the game. While it is not nearly as strong as it was before, conceptually, it is still game breaking and borderline uncounterable in necessary situations. You can’t attempt lunges against survivors that have it (or if you aren’t sure that they don’t) nor can you wait too long to swing due to your likely proximity to an undropped pallet. Smart survivors are aware of this and are capable of creating situations where you either eat a pallet stun or you hit them during Dead Hard while they are under the pallet and then they use that time to create distance. In the right hands, it is essentially a Styptic Agent that can be used multiple times.
Putting it plainly, this game is not designed or balanced around three health state chases . A single Dead Hard during any stage of the game can quite literally cause you to permanently lose control of an entire match. If you still commit to the chase, it’s extremely likely you’re going to lose two generators for it. However, if you leave that chase, you now have to commit to chasing survivors that are healthy while also not knowing if they have Dead Hard, as well. This is after crossing the map to contest those generators with your poor map mobility.
When looking at every single one of these elements, it should be clear that the reason tunneling is necessary is not for any other reason but due to there being no better option . These details I highlighted present no other form of viable strategy to counter them. It’s not even that tunneling is a viable counter in these scenarios. Even if you tunnel, survivors that are adequately equipped who also have gen-efficient teammates are likely still going to make winning extremely difficult for Clown on a large variety of maps. Choosing not to tunnel or pressure a hooked survivor is not suddenly going to give you good map mobility. All you’re doing is spending more time not actively engaged with any survivors as you slowly cross the map. During that time, gen-efficient survivors are finishing your generators. There’s just not enough generators to be completed by survivors who know how to do them for any other strategy to work.

Knowing WHEN to Tunnel Properly!
Now that it has been discussed as to why tunneling is so necessary for Clown in order to have a better chance of consistently winning your matches, I want to spend a good amount of time talking about how one can better do it properly. Believe it or not, tunneling is actually not that straight forward – especially if one is committing to proxy-camping in order to tunnel the unhooked survivor easier. There are a lot of dynamics that come into play which determine exactly when tunneling is most optimal. In some situations, tunneling can actually be detrimental in the event that survivors capitalize on what they have going for them. Let’s take a look at some factors and scenarios that should better exemplify what I mean.
Scenario 1:
You start the match and using your game sense you identify the likely possible spawn locations of survivors within the first 3 seconds. You head to one of these possible locations and two survivors disperse rather early. However, you happen to find a Meg hiding behind a rock. She gives you the first health state at 15 seconds into the match (it took you 12 seconds to get there) and you down her 8 seconds afterward. By the time you recover from weapon animation, pick her up, and hook her, another 8 seconds has passed. We are now at 31 seconds into the match.
Does one tunnel here? : Possibly , but there’s very little reason to take the risk. Let me explain.
First things first – during this whole time, at best, only one survivor at best was actively working on a generator during these first 31 seconds of the match. Two survivors were pushed off and Meg was immersing. This means that generators should have very little progress (if at all; even less if you brought Corrupt Intervention ) by the time you hook the Meg. If Clown were to leave Meg on the hook and try to immediately initiate a chase with another survivor, he could push survivors off of generators at the bare minimum while also encouraging Meg’s teammates to go for an unhook. This gives Clown two different possibilities for creating more pressure early that don’t involve tunneling Meg off the hook and risking the chance of bodyblocks and anti-tunnel perks. 1. Clown could either commit to the chase with the one survivor he pushed off a generator, or 2. he could return to the hook and try to capitalize on the unhooker being caught out of position for a much faster chase.
Either of these choices do not risk the possibility of slowing down Clown’s next hook state and playing into the universal advantage that survivors have over Clown in macro. If Meg happens to have anti-tunnel perks and you commit through them early into a regretfully-extended chase, the next hook state her teammates are likely to let her sit on the hook for the extended 70 second hook timer in order to maximize their repair progress. This can force you into a net lose/lose situation if the remaining three survivors play even better in chase, utilize pre-running, and optimize their generator efficiency. Instead of winning that match, there’s a possibility that you may only get two kills at best.
The choice to tunnel Meg is purely a risk you have to ask yourself if you’re willing to take. If Meg doesn’t have anti-tunnel perks and is unhooked fairly early, choosing to tunnel her if she’s caught out right when the unhook happens can result in you snowballing to where you win the match with 2-3 generators remaining. Even if her teammates decide they want to let her sit for longer, Meg being on death hook with no anti-tunnel means that you can now force the unhook by committing to a chase with any other survivor. As a team, survivors cannot afford to let you have two survivors hooked simultaneously. If you hook one of Meg’s teammates, they will have to unhook her on death hook (even though she has no anti-tunnel perks) and you can now take her out of the match.
In my opinion, if you have this much early pressure, I’d suggest trying to get an early injury on a survivor working on a generator before returning to the hook right as the unhook is likely to happen and focus whoever it was that unhooked Meg. This will give you an alternative target besides Meg in the event that you either can’t find her or catch her later in addition to giving you the most value out of your limited time and mobility. Hooking whoever unhooked Meg now requires a third survivor to stop repairing generators and locate Meg in order to heal her. Otherwise, she becomes a much easier target for you after you hooked her teammate  because even if she has anti-tunnel perks they will have expired in duration. It also makes it more difficult for Meg to coordinate with her teammates in hook save attempts since she risks the possibility that you just abandon the hook entirely and go for her.
What I want to emphasize the most as to what you should NOT be doing in this scenario is that you should NOT be committing to camping the hook to the degree where it’s a stalemate. With how quickly Meg was hooked, choosing to camp to such a level where all you’re doing is stalling out the unhook until the very last second isn’t going to prevent Meg’s teammates from unhooking her before she hits her second hook state. All you would be doing is conceding the early macro pressure that you have by allowing the survivors to overcome you in generator progress. That extra 10 seconds per hook state does wonders for survivor generator efficiency. That’s not even factoring in Reassurance – which I have found to be much more common following the 10 second hook state timer buff. Choosing to camp like this can absolutely lose you the game despite the speed of your first chase – especially if Meg happens to have anti-tunnel perks. You’re likely to lose all of your generators within 6 minutes even without the other survivors having toolboxes or generator perks if they recognize what you’re doing.
Scenario 2:
You’re having a rough early game.You may have identified where survivors spawned within the first 3 seconds of the match, but after spending 12 seconds heading to that location you discover that every survivor has pre-run from your location. You then spend another 12-15 seconds heading to a different location only to find a Jeff who has also decided to pre-run as you get closer to them. You decide to commit to the chase because you have to commit to something. The pre-run extends the time until you both happen to be within the same tile by 10-12 seconds. It then takes you another 7-10 seconds to get a hit around the pre-dropped pallet after setting up your bottles. You are now at 50-52 seconds for your first health state. Given that every other survivor pre-ran and is still healthy, you’re forced to commit to this chase otherwise you risk every other survivor doing the exact same thing. The injured Jeff runs to a strong tile and forces you to kick a pallet. That’s another 10-12 seconds. You finally catch them 15 seconds after kicking the pallet and spend 5-7 seconds recovering from weapon animation and carrying them to a hook. By this point, you will have achieved your first hook 80-86 seconds after the match has started.
Does one tunnel here? : Absolutely . There is no question. Allow me to explain.
Due to the base hook timers now being 70 seconds, it is almost a guarantee that you will lose 2 generators at some point within Jeff’s first hook stage. Regardless of whether you actively camp the hook or not, his teammates will finish their generators before they even begin to consider rotating across the map for an unhook. The fact that pre-running counters Clown’s poor map mobility so well means that your delayed first hook time gives you no leverage toward actually contesting these generators from being completed.
This is a big reason as to why Corrupt Intervention is so necessary on Clown if one were seeking consistent wins. In this scenario, with Corrupt Intervention, whichever generators survivors were working on wouldn’t have nearly as much progress due to survivors not having the opportunity to repair whichever ones they spawned on at match start. All of this being said, it is still besides the point. Since Corrupt Intervention is not basekit, one will likely not always be playing Clown with the perk in every single one of their builds since sometimes people like to seek creative variety in their loadouts. That is moreso where my reasoning here applies.
If you know for a fact that you can’t prevent survivors from finishing their generators before they unhook Jeff, you have to do what you can to encourage that the unhook still happens as soon as possible and that you are relatively within range of the unhook whenever survivors choose to do so. In this scenario, you have to double-down on the risk of anti-tunnel because Jeff being injured and out of position (regardless of anti-tunnel) makes him still the most vulnerable target. If Jeff happens to have Decisive Strike , even though it stalls out your second hook onto him, he can only use it once. This makes killing him after his second hook becomes dramatically faster and easier. It also means you can possibly force the second unhook onto Jeff sooner if you happen to catch someone else who is out of position.
One of the reasons as to why tunneling Jeff is so important in this type of scenario is that with this level of survivor chase and generator efficiency you aren’t playing into the possibility that you will be killing three survivors or more. Instead, tunneling Jeff ensures your best chance of getting two kills and six hooks at the bare minimum.
Choosing to spread hooks or focus on other survivors only gives the survivor team a chance of finishing all remaining generators before you’ve achieved your first sacrifice. Assuming you happen to hit endgame before you get your first kill, at best, you’re likely not getting anything more than that. This is also assuming that the other survivors don’t coordinate a guaranteed unhook escape due to basekit Borrowed Time providing both haste and protection. It is extremely difficult to borderline impossible for Clown to defend a hooked survivor against three coordinated and healthy survivor teammates. Thus, by killing at least one survivor before all generators are completed, this means that, at worst, Clown can secure a second kill even if the survivor wasn’t someone who was initially hooked in the first place.
Scenario 3:
You’re not having a fantastic early game but not the worst, either. Like usual, you identify possible survivor spawns within the first 3 seconds of the match. After spending 10-12 seconds heading to a survivor location, you accidentally happen across a Nancy who chose not to immerse in the corner but next to the pallet of a strong tile. As you approach her without being fully aware of your presence, she pre-drops the pallet from an advantageous position and leaves the tile expecting you to kick it. Instead, you rotate around the pallet and using your bottles hit her between the next tile 7-10 seconds later. Nancy is able to make it to another strong tile and, with another strong pre-drop, stalls out getting hit a second time by another 15-20 seconds afterward. From there, you spend 5-7 seconds recovering from your weapon animation, picking Nancy up, and carrying her to a hook. By this point, you will be 40-52 seconds into the match as you get your first hook state.
Does one tunnel here? : Not necessarily . It’s an option, but I would still try to hook another survivor after Nancy, first. Here’s my reasoning.
Within the timeframe of 40-52 seconds of a match starting, at best, survivors will have completed one generator by having two work on one together alongside another generator being at 45-50% repair progress. Assuming they all decide to split on individual generators, then no generators will be completed before the first hook. This gives Clown plenty of time to attempt locating a second survivor early enough into the match to force the other two survivors off of their generators before they are fully repaired. Even if Nancy is unhooked shortly before Clown hooks his second survivor, the limited duration of anti-tunnel perks means that Clown can confidently focus Nancy after hooking her teammate without risking anti-tunnel perks from coming into play. In addition, it makes it difficult for Nancy’s previously-hooked teammate to coordinate with the other survivors in saving her since they could similarly all risk you abandoning the hook and focusing her teammate in Nancy’s place. Once two survivors are on death hook, it becomes dramatically easier for Clown to snowball a win since not only can these survivors not coordinate together, but it gives Clown more impetus to commit to a chase with these survivors knowing that it will directly result in removing said survivor from the match.

Gideon Meat Plant
Map Size: 7/10
Chase Potential: 5-7/10
Required # of Kicked Pallets: 5-7 max (On average); 10 true maximum
Recommended Addons: Flask of Bleach + Cigar Box; Cheap Gin B.
Gideon Meat Plant has a reputation for being a map that is extremely difficult for most M1 killers. Clown, in particular, is suggested to struggle pretty badly on Gideon due to its sheer pallet density. Depending on the caliber of the survivors, this may be the case for you. If you decide to kick every pallet that is dropped, you are going to lose a lot of generators in the process with very few (if any) hooks in between. There are just too many pallets on the map to kick every single one – even if you want to play around the late game. Playing around the late game on Gideon isn’t even really viable, either, on account of the exit gates being uncontestable. That’s not to say that you won’t still be kicking a lot of pallets. However, you need to be capable of adapting against multiple instances of pre-dropped pallets across different tiles by knowing how to execute double-bottle combos. Further, you have to be sure that the pallets you spend time kicking are only done in areas where you expect to contest survivors multiple times in chase. This is to ensure that the time you spend kicking pallets is effective pressure.
As far as the map’s structure, it has its advantages and disadvantages. It is a vertical map that isn’t too large horizontally. There are multiple staircases throughout the map and multiple holes that allow you to drop down to the bottom floor. Compared to other vertical maps, even Midwich, traversal from the top to the bottom floor feels much more fluid. If you can get a hook on the bottom floor, it’s not hard to put pressure on generators on the top floor before proxying back to the bottom floor when an unhook takes place. Many of the generator spawns can be close to each other but on separate floors, which makes tracking repair progress simultaneously much easier. It also makes it easier to play into a 3-gen situation if possible and necessary.
However, the tiles themselves aren’t exactly conventional. Some tiles are corridors , some are open rooms , and others are closed rooms that become open when a nearby generator is completed. Depending on how the tiles are strung together, you can end up wasting a lot of crucial time taking really inefficient chases during the early game which snowballs into a lot of pressure against you. As more generators get completed, more closed rooms become open . While some of these rooms are still unsafe once they are open, they are significantly more unsafe while they are closed due to tile-slicing .
Corridor tiles are very one-dimensional. They are mostly just a pathway that connects to another tile of either the same or a different category. You can almost always count on any corridor tile on the map having at least one pallet that is meant to physically block your path. At least two corridors have two pallets each . One of those tiles is right next to the bathroom. When a survivor takes you to these locations, you either have to kick every single one of these pallets or you have to abandon chase. Most room tiles – open or closed – are either directly connected to a corridor tile or have one nearby. When deciding whether or not it’s worth it to spend time putting pressure on a corridor tile with multiple strong pallets, it’s important to consider if that corridor tile is nearby a different unsafe tile and would afterward leave survivors vulnerable. Further, you have to be sure that the time you spend committing to a chase at said area of the map is worth risking the possibility of survivors finishing a generator someplace else and making that area safer for them in exchange.
The open room tiles are less safe for survivors but still provide them with some opportunity to escape if you misplay and they have efficient movement. Depending on the shape of the pallet tile, it should be possible to hit a survivor either around the loop as they run to the pallet or as they abandon the tile altogether. There are a few open room tiles with non-loopable safe pallets that will require you to kick the pallet. Some of these pallets you can mind game a survivor by faking a pallet kick in order to get them to abandon it. The execution of a double-bottle combo as you fake a pallet kick can trick survivors into making themselves vulnerable before they realize what you’re doing. Smart survivors that won’t fall for this will require you to kick the pallet. You can ensure they don’t create a lot of distance when this happens by executing the same double-bottle combo as before.
Compared to the other tiles, closed room tiles are the most unsafe areas on the map. They typically have only one doorway to get in and out of the tile that is initially available. This allows you to put very strong pressure on survivors at these tiles very early into the game. Some closed room tiles are jungle gyms, others are unsafe pallet loops. The shape doesn’t matter; there is very little survivors can do as long as you slice the tile by the open doorway. It is important to make sure you slice the tile properly . Most closed room tiles are connected to either an open room or a strong corridor. These open rooms or strong corridors can also be connected to other open rooms or strong corridors. If you allow a survivor the opportunity to escape a closed room tile – especially early into the game – there is a very good chance that they can waste a lot of your time by dropping strong pallets.
Although I have it listed that I consider Gideon Meat Plant to be of a favorable size with moderate chase potential, it is probably one of the few (if only) maps that I consider to be extremely add-on dependent . Both Flask of Bleach and Cigar Box together are highly recommended in order to reach maximum chase potential. Without Flask of Bleach , there are some certain loops that become too tight to hit a survivor around a pre-dropped pallet. At least two of the examples below require Flask of Bleach in order to be successful. Being forced to kick more pallets gives survivors more leeway to create distance to other tiles. The more time you spend without getting downs, the faster generators get completed and the more closed rooms become open – making it even harder for you to build any lasting pressure. Cigar Box helps ensure that you don’t spend a lot of time out of chases by providing you with aura reading across both floors. It also gives you necessary information for setting up your double-bottle combos around high wall tiles. Being able to see survivor auras on the other side of high wall tiles means that you only have to worry about efficient bottle placement and movement in order to hit a survivor around a pre-dropped pallet. Without Cigar Box , it is possible that a survivor uses the high walls in order to mind game you with their movement and can possibly even find an opening to abandon the tile.
The only downside with this add-on combination is that you only have 4 bottles and have to be extremely efficient with your bottle usage. Misplaced bottles can result in you likely not having enough bottles necessary in order to set up a double-bottle combo without reloading. Assuming that the survivor has an opportunity to break away from a tile, they will likely be seeking to take it once you start reloading. This can be very problematic if the circumstances aren’t available for you to slice the tile in a way that keeps them trapped behind the pallet.
You typically can’t afford to reload as you are chasing a survivor between tiles because your reduced movement speed while reloading is going to result in them creating too much distance. This is doubly-so on account of the fact that you only have 4 bottles. Using yellow bottles to close the distance that you lost from reloading as you are chasing a survivor between tiles is counter-intuitive to the purpose of winning that chase. Under these circumstances, you are going to be recreating the very same problem that you had prior when you run out of bottles again at that next tile assuming that you can’t slice that tile as well.
Without both add-ons, it should still be possible to do well as long as you have at least one of them and the survivors aren’t of a high caliber with strong builds and coordination. Cheap Gin Bottle combined with Cigar Box performs very well since the extra bottles give you a lot of flexibility. Instead of having to reload more frequently between health states, you are able to follow up on an injury and down a survivor much faster. Further, the extra bottles allow you to take advantage of using yellow bottles to find survivors and start chases without being as concerned with not having enough bottles remaining to hit the survivor before you reload.
Flask of Bleach and Cheap Gin Bottle is also another strong combo for a similar reason, but has its own disadvantages, as well. You are able to maintain consistent pressure around even the strongest of loops without needing to reload between health states or kick as many pallets. However, the lack of aura reading is going to be very punishing against cunning survivors that either know how to hide their presence or can mind game at high wall tiles.
C-Table Pallet Tech
Video Example
This room is arguably one of the roughest areas for Clown on the entire map. Not only are there two incredibly strong pallets, but this room is always connected to the bathroom tile below it through a hole in the corner. Thus, trapping a survivor in this room is very difficult. So far, I’ve found only one viable way in this room where you can reliably slice the tile .
First, it should be mentioned that the previous pallet we passed next to the small staircase walkway is practically non-loopable . If this previous pallet is dropped , you should be looking to cut off both the doorway out of the room and the pathway to the hole in the corner using pink bottles before you kick the pallet. Depending on the distance between you and the survivor when you’re approaching this room, it should be practical to have already thrown your yellow bottle for this setup prematurely. Looking at the video as an example, the yellow bottle that I used to chase Nea to the C-pallet was also placed in a way where I could have benefited from it if I needed to kick the previous pallet. I would merely need to throw one pink bottle each to the left and right of the pallet right before moving back to kick it.
In order to trap a survivor in this room using tile-slicing , you need to push them to the C-pallet up against the back wall. While this pallet is rather safe for survivors, it carries a lot of risk from being next to a wall at the corner of the room. Safety at this loop for the survivor hinges on proper positioning. Further, the optimal strategy for survivor positioning changes depending on whether or not Clown plans to kick the pallet. If a survivor is too close to the pallet when Clown kicks it, he is likely to hit them before they reach another pallet assuming he executed a double-bottle combo. Conversely, if a survivor runs too far away from the pallet and Clown doesn’t kick it, that survivor becomes vulnerable to getting hit before they can run back to the pallet.
This tech essentially lets you turn this strong pre-dropped pallet into a 50/50 where you can get a very quick health state without losing a lot of time. It is still somewhat dangerous due to the possibility of a survivor correctly guessing whether or not you actually kick the pallet. Further, the dependency on Flask of Bleach might make attempting this tech undesirable. Even so, it is being included due to its viability as a risk-reward strategy. The prevalence of this type of mind-game also extends beyond this particular tile within this one map. While the required execution in this one example may be rather strict, other scenarios may offer you additional leeway.

1. My chase with Nea already began from another room. Seeing that she was running to this corner of the map with two strong unused pallets, I purposefully threw a yellow bottle with anticipation that she was going to pre-drop the previous pallet. I was surprised when she didn’t pre-drop the pallet. However, when I originally threw that yellow bottle, I also did so with this contingency in mind. In turn, my yellow bottle was active right as I passed through it. This allowed me to close enough distance to keep her away from dropping down the hole into the bathroom. Instead, I was able to force her to the C-pallet and slice the tile . 2. Since I still had three bottles left, I threw another yellow bottle without delay on my side of the tables away from the pallet. While that yellow bottle was inactive, I strafed left-to-right with my remaining haste buff in order to put pressure on Nea’s positioning. Standing still while your yellow gas on the floor is inactive is typically unwise. There is no reason not to strafe as you want to keep survivors second guessing your intentions. Strafing makes you harder to read due to the proactive movement.
After my second strafe to the right, I knew that my second yellow was almost ready to be active. 3. As I strafed to the left for the third time, I then turned and threw a pink bottle behind Nea on the other side of the tables. Immediately after, I walked through my now-active yellow gas behind the pallet on my side of the loop and looked down. If I actually kicked the pallet, I likely would’ve extended the chase by at least 15-20+ seconds depending on if she could find another pallet in time. There is still another strong pallet in this room alone, so the chances of that happening are extremely possible.
When it comes to looking down in order to fake a pallet kick, it’s important to make sure you time it properly. You don’t want to leave the pallet too early, as that means survivors are going to create less distance before they realize that you’re chasing them from the other side. Similarly, you don’t want to wait too long, as experienced survivors will recognize that you aren’t actually kicking the pallet and will run back to its safety. You want to simulate the amount of time that it takes in your head for Clown to hit the pallet with his foot for the first time. After that amount of time has passed – and not a second longer – that is when you want to break away from the pallet and flank them from the other side of the loop.
4. Once I was sure that the appropriate amount of time had passed, I moved away from the pallet and ran through my second yellow gas again as I chased her around the other side of the loop. Nea was under the impression that I was going to kick the pallet and had already begun moving to the previous pallet on the other side of the room. In the process, she ended up running through my pink gas during her attempt to get there. As I started approaching her from the other side of the loop, she ended up having to double-back through my pink gas a second time in order to get back to the pallet. The difference in speed from my yellow and pink bottles along with the proper timing of my fake pallet kick meant that I was just barely able to hit her as she was sliding over the pallet.
G-Tile Trap (Closed/Open Room)
Video Example
Don’t be fooled by the size of this pallet loop. While it may look intimidating, it’s surprisingly both very loopable and very mind gameable. Your mileage may vary slightly without Flask of Bleach when it comes to anti-looping around the back corner. Catching a survivor off of a pallet vault, however, isn’t very difficult if you run the tables tightly while sped up by a yellow bottle (no add-ons required). The animation lock after a survivor vaults a pallet means that you should be able to hit them before they vault back if you lunge from the correct angle. If you want to play around this pre-dropped pallet , the most important concern you should have when you and a survivor end up at this tile is that you do not make any mistakes . Even though survivors are vulnerable to getting hit, this tile punishes you very hard if you miscalculate your distance.
Part of what makes this tile unsafe for survivors is that it always spawns in the exact same place at a corner of the map . The less variance there is in a tile’s location, the easier it is to develop consistent strategies with your bottles that you can apply across multiple matches. This being said, this tile does possess some variance in the form of an open/closed doorway.
Depending on if both doors are open or closed, the tile becomes either more/less safe for survivors and significantly more/less punishing for Clown (especially if you miss your lunge). When both doors are open, approaching a survivor from the left side of the long table means that if you miss your lunge, the survivor can vault the pallet and run out of the room. If this happens, you’re going to be forced to kick the pallet just to chase after them (removing the trap) while also losing a lot of distance. Throw boons and medkits into the mix, and you’re going to feel really pressured not to break chase (which likely may cost you gens).
Regardless of both doorways being open or closed, the fact that this is a corner tile means that it is extremely vulnerable to tile-slicing . When you have a survivor positioned inside this loop, you want to stand on the outside of the long tables fairly close to both doorways. This way, the survivor doesn’t have any ability to abandon the tile through either the freezer room corridor or the open room staircase. You can then use this as an opportunity to perform a free reload without having to worry about a survivor creating distance. From there, it’s all about proper execution of tactics within your course of action .

Midwich Elementary School
Map Size: 8/10
Chase Potential: 9/10
Required # of Kicked Pallets: 2-3 max (On average); 4 true maximum
Compared to any other map in the game, this one is arguably Clown’s best. It is a vertical map, but horizontally it is not too big. As such, the reliance on using the staircases to go upstairs isn’t that much of a detriment. Going downstairs is significantly easier. On the second floor by both exit gates are a total of 2 window vaults and 5 open holes that give you multiple avenues for reaching the first floor without taking the staircases. One of the sides with an exit gate also has a classroom with a collapsed pathway to traverse between the first and second floor. If you can get a survivor hooked on the first floor, this means that you can go upstairs to apply pressure before dropping right back down to contest the unhook and snowball even further.
Spawns across this map are very consistent. Along with what was stated above, everything that is listed below is going to be a static occurrence in every single match that you play:
*All but 2(?) classrooms have a pallet that does not need to be broken .
*Staircases are always in opposite corners of the map.
*One hallway by a staircase has a guaranteed pallet spawn that needs to be broken .
*The top of one staircase has a window. The other staircase has a third staircase which leads to a dead-end.
*There is a path upstairs blocked by two breakable walls in the bathroom.
*The bathroom has a guaranteed pallet spawn that usually has to be broken .
*The infirmary next to one of the exit gates has a guaranteed pallet spawn that needs to be broken.
*The center of the map has a clock tower with two guaranteed pallet spawns. Usually, at least one of the two pallets needs to be broken. You can mindgame around the unbroken pallet.
*At least one classroom on both the first and second floor is connected to another classroom by a window vault. These classrooms are denoted by the breakable walls that are present from the hallway and must be broken. You can mindgame the window vault using yellows for lunge.
*The secretary desk next to one of the exit gates has a window vault and a breakable wall that must be broken .
While accessibility between both floors and most spawns having static consistency are strong positives to the map, the greatest aspect of Midwich for Clown is the sheer chase potential. Every single classroom pallet on the entire map is unsafe against Clown even when pre-dropped. Combining your yellow + pink bottles together means they will not make it back to the pallet before they get hit. As a result, you never have to worry about survivors having the advantage over you once they abandon the hallways to go into a classroom.
The pallets themselves are not only unsafe for looping. If a survivor chooses to go behind a classroom pallet, the Clown can use positioning to trap them within the classroom and guarantee a free reload. Every classroom has three openings (with one of those sometimes being a window). Clown standing at the center on the opposite side of the classroom pallet means that if a survivor attempts to abandon the classroom, the Clown can cancel the reload and hit them before they can reach a safe obstacle. Finishing the reload also means you get a guaranteed hit if they try to leave the classroom against your double-bottle combo or if they try to loop the pallet.
If they try to run forward down the hallways, you can close a lot of distance combining yellows + pinks effortlessly. Other than the one guaranteed pallet spawn that blocks a staircase or the window that spawns next to a staircase on the second floor, there’s not much to protect survivors from getting hit quickly here, either. Any other option taken – such as running into a classroom or dropping down a hole from the second floor – not only ensures that a hit is inevitable, but at best it delays that hit by 10 seconds (assuming that a reload isn’t necessary).
The center courtyard is for the most part unsafe. The hedge tiles with either the window or pallet spawn require some double-bottle combo setup but isn’t too difficult. The clock tower, however, is one of the safest areas on the entire map. It has two guaranteed pallet spawns. If you choose to chase a survivor here, you can usually get away with kicking only one pallet and mindgaming them with a yellow bottle around the other one. However, choosing not to chase them here is also an option assuming that they don’t have a boon nearby or strong medkits.
Choosing to end a chase here usually requires more time than it would at any other area of the map while also pulling you away from generators that you need to defend. The difference between ending a chase at the clock tower compared to a classroom is that you are more positioned to hook a survivor near a generator (and thus contest multiple objectives at once) if you down them in a classroom. Downing a survivor near the clock tower is still not a bad outcome. Because of the multiple ways to drop from the second floor near both exit gates, you can contest any unhook on the first floor at the center much more easily than you could if the unhook was also on the first floor but in a hallway.
The main drawback to Midwich is that sometimes you get a bad spread of hook spawns. While everything else about Midwich is pretty static, the hook spawns are not one of them. As a result, you may have some matches where survivors go down in an area of the map where you only have 1 hook available (if at all). Without running Agitation, it’s suggested if you’re not presently aware of any nearby hooks to use a yellow bottle on pickup as a layer of added security.

The intent for this section is to guide people toward making their own strong builds by giving them a foundation of understanding on strong perks that they can work with. Further, dissuading people from taking weaker perks that one might initially consider to be stronger helps ensure that they branch out in a more successful direction. All of this being said, I still encourage anyone and everyone to find different ways to think outside the box. Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance (Pain Resonance)
At one point, it was fair to say that Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance was a staple primary regression perk in Clown’s arsenal. It used to immediately provide a very moderate and consistent decrease to repair progress on whichever generator needed it the most with the only contingency being that you need to hook a survivor on 1 of 4 designated hooks. This made it very effective on Clown, as a lack of mobility makes it difficult for him to apply any form of regression or slowdown if he is required to cross the map in order to do so. 15% regression was also nothing to scoff at, either, as it was the equivalent of 13.5 seconds of repair time needed for one survivor.
With the changes in the 6.7 patch, the role of Pain Resonance shifted in a very interesting way. Instead of being a primary regression perk, it became a secondary regression perk. What this means is that one should be running Pain Resonance in your build as a means to supplement another regression perk that has a more consistent and applicable effect. Relying on Pain Resonance as a primary (and possibly, only) regression perk is no longer practical due to how few uses of it are available. Being required to spread hooks also limits its ability to be used in this manner. You are still likely going to be better off tunneling out one survivor instead of trying to get more than 1 use out of it early, as survivors are more gen-efficient at a baseline when more of them are still alive. This is especially true with the regression nerf this perk received sometime afterward.
The increase and then decrease of instant regression from 15% to 25% back to 20% is very significant with respect to its overall viability. After the nerf, that is the equivalent of only 18 seconds added to a generator’s repair time for one survivor without considering any other modifiers – an increase of only 4.5 seconds added to the original baseline. When staggered to be applied at the proper moment, this regression can be fairly punishing on a team that is not positioned to undo it. As such, it requires a little more tactical planning in order to get the most value out of it. It is definitely not a bad perk on Clown after the change, but the decrease from 25% regression to 20% really hurt its effectiveness.
When not used in a practical manner, Pain Resonance is capable of providing such little value that it is basically a wasted perk slot. With only four total uses of Pain Resonance divided between one use per survivor, you cannot afford to trigger Pain Resonance at a bad time. One common mistake some killers make is that they trigger Pain Resonance immediately after a generator is completed – resulting in much less lost progress on a generator. This wasn’t nearly as punishing whenever you had the ability to trigger Pain Resonance multiple times on the same survivor. After the changes in 6.7, you cannot afford to let this happen at a bad time in order for the perk to remain effective.
Recognizing how to get the most value out of Pain Resonance requires a deeper knowledge of the game’s general pacing as to recognize both when you should be applying Pain Resonance as well as what you should be doing immediately after. This is highly dependent on a variety of external factors – such as how far away from you is the generator hit with Pain Res, how many survivors are still alive, whether or not two survivors were working on it, if they’re injured or healthy, the position of your three closest generators, their exhaustion perks, the map itself, and more. As such, qualifying explicitly what it is that you should be doing in each isolated incident is not something that can be reasonably explained within this guide. It is on you, the reader, to take into account these external factors for yourself in order to reach that conclusion. This is why I do not recommend one run it casually , as it is a perk that necessitates an acquired understanding of the game itself. If you feel that you are lacking in areas of that understanding, your usage of Pain Resonance has the potential to be sub-optimal.
Assuming it is utilized as a supplementary regression perk, Pain Resonance pairs decently with other instant regression perks, such as Pop Goes the Weasel and Jolt . Survivors who touch the Pain Ress’d generator in order to stop the passive regression enable you to follow up on that generator with Pop Goes the Weasel. Similarly, it is possible to apply both Jolt and Pain Resonance to the same generator even if a survivor doesn’t tap it since you’re not required to kick it. This gives you two different ways where you can strategically double-up Pain Resonance with another perk that serves as your primary regression. Whenever you know you cannot or should not apply Pain Resonance, your other perk exists to give you at least some form of pressure.
That being said, instant regression overall isn’t looking to be all that favorable going forward from this new patch. Every instant regression perk other than Pain Resonance has, to date, been significantly nerfed to either provide less regression than it previously had or some other weakened form of utility. Pop Goes the Weasel, in particular, suffers from requiring you to walk to the generator and kick it within a short period of time in order to get value. Personally, I would consider other options as a primary slowdown perk over instant regression.
When it comes to finding a better primary generator/slowdown perk to pair with Pain Resonance, previously I’d sooner consider running Deadlock over either of those two previously mentioned choices ( Pop Goes the Weasel and Jolt ). However, Deadlock also received a nerf sometime afterward, reducing its duration from 30 seconds to 25 seconds. With this nerf to Deadlock, I consider either three choices to be essentially equal with their own pros and cons. Some people might think that Deadlock and Pain Resonance have anti-synergy, but that is actually much less true with the 6.7 changes. In fact, Pain Resonance could be arguably weaker if it were changed to have lower regression at the expense of its scourge hook status so that it could be applied to every first hook. Using game sense, it can be possible to determine whether or not the value one would gain from applying Pain Resonance would or would not be wasted when Deadlock is also one of your perks. Deadlock enables one to determine whether or not they should be looking for a scourge hook or if they should hook a survivor on a normal hook, instead. Depending on the remaining duration of Deadlock, one could also stagger hooking a survivor on a scourge hook until immediately after Deadlock expires in order to ensure that they get full value from both perks. This type of synergy normally went unrecognized on account of Pain Resonance having much more consistent value in addition to its effect being significantly weaker.
Another interesting choice that one could run as a flex perk would also be Agitation . People used to run Agitation in order to ensure that they could hook a survivor on a scourge hook every time . Now, with the changes, Agitation can be used to ensure that you have the choice of whether or not to hook a survivor on either a normal or scourge hook. Sometimes, you might be forced into hooking a survivor on a hook you do not prefer either due to raw distance from the hook or survivor interference. Agitation would make these situations less common while also giving you the most control over using Pain Resonance properly. While some people might think that Clown’s yellow bottles would do a similar job as Agitation, the speed of Agitation itself is much greater and doesn’t require any setup or resource expenditure (which affects your momentum).
Looking at anti-synergies, I actually think it could be argued that Pain Resonance no longer synergizes with other scourge hook perks – such as Floods of Rage or Gift of Pain . Given that Floods of Rage and Gift of Pain aren’t limited in how many times that they can be used, you’re essentially encouraged to always seek out scourge hooks with every hook whenever you’re running either of these two perks. However, this conflicts with Pain Resonance due to the strategic implication of wanting to sometimes seek out normal hooks as to not trigger your limited Pain Resonance uses at a bad time. While Floods of Rage and Gift of Pain can still be run together, I definitely advise against running either of them alongside Pain Resonance.
Zanshin Tactics (Zanshin)
This perk is probably, in my opinion, the most pivotal information/chase perk in Clown’s arsenal. I would honestly go as far as to say that running this perk is one of two perks (the other being Coup de Grace ) that are 100% necessary in any build designed with the express purpose of consistently winning your matches. When attempting to properly utilize Clown’s power at any high wall tile – especially on indoor maps – knowing the shape and structure of a tile as early as possible is absolutely vital . If you aren’t immediately aware of certain details of a tile as you approach it, you can end up either waiting too long to set up your double bottle combo or accidentally set it up improperly. As a result, you will likely then lose momentum in a chase that may ultimately decide whether or not you are able to hit a survivor before they reach an area of safety – such as on the other side of a window or un-dropped pallet. The more momentum you lose, the longer a chase lasts on average and, in turn, there is less pressure created even if you are able to successfully down and hook a survivor.
Outside of a few maps like Midwich Elementary School, the way a map is generated at the start of every match is random to some variable degree. When you play on the same map multiple times, you may experience anything from certain key tiles spawning in different locations to jungle gyms with different shapes, pallets, and window spawns. In turn, I personally believe that when speaking about a large majority of the maps, it is practically impossible to consistently perform as well using Clown’s power without running Zanshin Tactics .
That’s not to say that you can’t ever perform well in your matches if you don’t run this perk. However, you are absolutely going to find yourself in some of your matches either being surprised by the existence of a window, the placement of a pallet, or the convenience of another nearby tile. I have watched a handful of skilled people play Clown – some of them with 5000+ hours on the game. Even with all of that experience, they still sometimes made mistakes in chase because they weren’t aware of the overall shape and structure of a tile ( this still holds true in 2025 ). This is not to be interpreted as a knock against anyone ; in fact, it is the opposite . No matter how much time you’ve invested into learning the game, without Zanshin Tactics , mistakes due to miscalculating random tile generation is just something that is going to happen on many maps due to how much variance takes place each time you play on them.
Understanding the value of Zanshin Tactics comes from recognizing exactly what information is being told to you while you run it and then knowing what to do with it. The information you gain from the perk is multi-faceted. It’s either directly telling you about the existence of a window or pallet, or it’s telling you the opposite . If you don’t see an aura for a window or a pallet at a high wall tile, that is the perk telling you when you set up your double-bottle combo/facilitate your course of action that you only need to play around the shape of that tile’s walls themselves and its proximity to other tiles. Conversely, once you approach a tile that has either a window, pallet spawn, or both, the information on their placement that is being relayed to you by the perk gives you the necessary context to act without letting those factors interfere with your decisions. Uncertainty leads to hesitance – which mentally impacts your execution (in turn, lowering your momentum).
With an active distance of 32 meters , Zanshin Tactics is extremely proactive in a chase against survivors as you move across multiple tiles. There shouldn’t be any surprise that takes place as you move from one tile to another due to the range that visible pallet/window auras are available. You should be using the information given to you by this perk to plan out your course of action in a way where you can counter a survivor even as they choose to abandon a tile. In many circumstances, it is possible to hit a survivor that attempts to abandon a tile before they reach an area of safety at another tile by speeding yourself up with a yellow bottle and slowing them down with a pink. Zanshin Tactics helps you identify the correct angles and direction in order to execute this properly without making any mistakes.
Another way that I like to describe Zanshin Tactics is that it is like a highlighter for your notes . As you get more experience playing Clown and learning how to perform double-bottle combos at different tiles, your skill in reflexively doing so improves, as well. With both the information given by Zanshin Tactics and the necessary reflexive skill, it becomes possible to quickly identify each situation and set up the proper double-bottle combo without cutting into your momentum.
Recently, Zanshin Tactics received an incredible buff and now also provides 5 seconds of aura reading on a survivor who drops a pallet! This change is extremely helpful when it comes to both maintaining as well as increasing momentum, as it plays into yellow + pink bottle pre-placement against survivors who choose to either camp or pre-drop pallets at high wall tiles and jungle gyms. To some degree, it also takes away from Cigar Box’s unique advantage mid-chase, as Zanshin’s aura reading is conveniently opportunistic enough to activate at just the right moments where one would otherwise be relying on Cigar Box for information.

# Coup de grâce (CDG)
While this perk might seem unorthodox to many – after hours of rigorous testing – I can confidently say that I am absolutely astounded and amazed by the influence it has had on winning my matches. It is borderline inarguable that this perk has single-handedly won me multiple entire games all on its own just due to its sheer snowball potential ( this is still true in 2025) . I am not exaggerating when I say it is that good. However, what is it that makes Coup de grâce not only viable on Clown, but extremely powerful? After all, Clown is an M1-only killer without any means of injuring a survivor beyond using his basic attack. The answer to this question can be found within the very nature of Clown’s power. Due to the sheer speed displacement provided by combining both of Clown’s bottles together, it becomes possible to preserve stacks of CDG to a degree that probably no other M1-only killer besides Clown is capable of doing. This enables CDG to essentially function for Clown as a toggle-ability in order to down survivors in situations that would otherwise never happen. As a survivor, playing around a Clown using his yellow and pink bottles together is already very difficult, but sometimes still possible. Throw CDG into the mix, and suddenly there is zero counterplay.
Although I may describe CDG to be very strong, I also want to emphasize that this perk requires an extremely invested and calculated understanding of Clown’s power in order to actualize value and preserve your stacks. I want to be very clear that I do NOT recommend this perk for either newer or even intermediate Clowns. I ONLY recommend CDG to extremely confident and advanced-level Clowns who regularly use both of their bottles in combination to a degree where they aren’t regularly kicking many pallets mid-chase. Without this caliber of skill, it becomes very likely that you will end up having to go for unnecessary lunges. Depending on the distance, these lunges could also be within the basic distance of a regular lunge (meaning that CDG loses a stack but you gain zero perk value from it).
Interestingly, the means of how I discovered CDG’s potential for Clown came following the Dead Hard nerfs in the 6.7 patch. After the update, I began to notice a significant drop in the number of survivors running Dead Hard due to its new stipulations. Using this information, I realized upon considering CDG for Clown that it might be simultaneously possible to both preserve stacks of CDG in addition to utilizing that extra lunge distance from it to down survivors with greater potential than I had without it. Assuming survivors don’t actually have Dead Hard, the ability to play in a similar style as if they did have it by walking up to them in order to preserve your stacks suddenly becomes more practical. Further, there isn’t any concern with survivors reacting to your long-range lunges using Dead Hard and giving themselves an extra health state. As a result, when Dead Hard isn’t a factor , Coup de grâce on Clown functionally doesn’t have any significant downsides with how it impacts your gameplay in chase. The upsides are also so great that it becomes very hard to argue why it shouldn’t be in every single one of your builds assuming you are able to utilize it properly. After what I have seen and learned, I would genuinely consider Coup de grâce to be a Top 3 perk on Clown at the bare minimum . It closes out whatever weaknesses he has in chase by giving you that assurance you need when you otherwise wouldn’t have it. This is especially important in 2025 , as with the current state of survivor efficiency and longer hook timers, Assurance is Premium .
A big part of what makes CDG strong on Clown when you use it has to do with the numbers behind lunging and Clown’s power. A standard lunge lasts for about 1 second. While lunging, Clown’s movement speed (as well as any other killer) is increased from 4.6 m/s to 6.9 m/s. By comparison, this is faster than the running speed of Victor with both Forest Stew and Madeleine’s Scarf . It is also faster than Legion with Mural Sketch and maximum chain hits even if he isn’t running Never Sleep Pills to make him initially move slower. Lunging in DBD is, thus, very powerful for securing hits. Being able to lunge for 80% longer translates into an extra 1.84 meters of lethality at base without factoring in either of Clown’s bottles. Once either pink or yellow bottles come into play, the threat of being hit becomes monumentally greater .
Both Clown’s yellow and pink bottles synergize with CDG in order to secure easier hits than ever before. Assuming they aren’t slowed down by a pink bottle, a survivor can travel 7.2 meters after 1.8 seconds (the duration of a CDG lunge). By using a pink bottle, you can reduce this distance down to 6.12 meters after 1.8 seconds with there still being some hindrance duration left over. This is a difference of 1.08 meters over the same period of time. Being slowed down by Clown’s pink bottles also forces survivors to medium vault – giving you an even greater potential for securing hits on semi-distant survivors. This is also without factoring in Flask of Bleach . If you happen to have Flask of Bleach, a survivor can only travel 5.83 meters after 1.8 seconds – a difference of 0.29 meters compared to without it. Depending on the size of the tile, this extra slowdown does add up and can be the difference between you hitting a survivor before they make it to a window or pallet.
Compared to how his pink bottles interact with CDG, Clown’s yellow bottles work a bit differently. A standard CDG lunge without being invigorated lets Clown travel 12.42 meters after 1.8 seconds. By simply using a yellow bottle, it becomes possible for Clown to travel 13.662 meters after 1.8 seconds – which amounts to a difference of 1.242 meters over the same period of time. It should be said that the duration of your yellow bottles does matter with respect to whether or not you will be invigorated before you lunge in some chases. Around shorter loops, duration-increasing add-ons such as Solvent Jug or Garish Makeup Kit aren’t really that necessary. However, these add-ons can be pivotal in ensuring that you’re still invigorated when you lunge as you chain between multiple tiles.
Based on the numbers, it appears that, individually, Clown’s yellow bottles are far more impactful for securing lunges with CDG unless the survivor being chased is running to a window. Being able to force a survivor to medium vault a window is likely to make a pink bottle better than a yellow bottle on a 1:1 ratio due to both the raw movement speed slowdown along with the extra time required to complete a medium vault action. That extra time is essentially raw distance for Clown to hit a survivor as they are vaulting. A fast vault takes 0.5 seconds, and a medium vault takes 0.9 seconds. Within that period of 0.4 seconds, a non-invigorated Clown is capable of traveling an extra 2.76 meters while lunging with CDG.
Everything described is also merely what is gained when using each of Clown’s bottles individually . Whenever possible, combining both yellow and pink bottles together with CDG is what allows this perk to perform at its maximum potential. Assuming that Clown is invigorated and a survivor is hindered by a pink bottle, everything previously mentioned adds up to total an absurd amount of free distance in chase. By subtracting the distance traveled by a slowed down survivor from the distance traveled by an invigorated Clown that is lunging after them with CDG, it is shown that Clown can get 7.54 meters closer to a survivor over as little as 1.8 seconds. Factoring in the extra time required for a medium vault, and that distance closed gets up to as much as 10.57 meters. Again, this is after subtracting the distance a survivor creates for themselves by attempting to run away from the Clown.
When running CDG in your builds, one should use their stacks often but not too aggressively. Two great examples of when to use it are to prevent a survivor from going down under a non-dropped pallet or when hitting a healthy survivor at an isolated tile so that they don’t get much distance off of the speed boost. After getting buffed, you are now given 10 TOTAL uses of CDG but you can only hold 5 of those uses at any given time. Further, you only gain two stacks of CDG as you become closer and closer to losing all of your generators. Liberal and unnecessary usage of CDG is likely to result in you not having stacks when you need it during a chase. It is also recommended for this reason that one adopts a very aggressive playstyle with the mindset of tunneling out survivors as quickly as possible, as roaming the map is already a poor macro decision to make on Clown due to his poor mobility. Tunneling goes hand-and-hand with CDG for this reason, as you will be initiating chases with injured survivors within your proximity who already become a priority for being taken out of the match due to their hook states. Without some form of endurance buff protection (such as Off the Record , Dead Hard , and Borrowed Time ) or bodyblocks from survivor teammates, CDG makes it incredibly easy to tunnel survivors off the hook and out of the match.
Dealing with these aforementioned survivor counterplay strategies isn’t too difficult on its own and can also be made easier through perk synergies. Sloppy Butcher and Forced Penance are excellent perks to use alongside CDG due to how they impact survivors who show up to protect their teammates.  If you are running either of these perks and survivors bodyblock inefficiently, it becomes possible for you to win the game solely due to poor decision-making on the survivor team’s part. Injured survivor teammates cannot bodyblock for another survivor mid-chase without going down and potentially opening up the team to get snowballed. They also cannot coordinate unhooks when you are camping, nor can they work on any generators near a hooked survivor’s location. This makes keeping survivors at the injured status very valuable when you have multiple stacks of CDG. It becomes practical to win by sheer slugging due to the absurd lethality of Clown’s power when also combined with CDG.
Countering survivor endurance buffs, sadly, isn’t as easy. To my knowledge, a killer perk that nullifies endurance either directly or indirectly doesn’t exist. In turn, one must compensate for this through specific decision-making relative to the prospective negative impact brought by the endurance buff in the event that it were allowed to gain value. As an example , if my first hook on a survivor came at a delay, I know that I will need to be nearby whenever the unhook were to happen on the off-chance that the unhooked survivor has Off the Record . Assuming I am not nearby and the unhooked survivor does have Off the Record, it is possible that they create plenty of early distance to a point where I am fundamentally chasing a survivor with two health states instead of one . Being close when the unhook happens enables me to apply deep wounds and break Off the Record early. Not only does this drastically reduce the overall chase time, but it also puts extra impetus on the unhooking survivor to provide cover for the person they unhooked. Otherwise, the whole team bears the expense of me taking advantage of their rescue. This pulls them away from generators and provides me with free slowdown.

Genetic Limits (GL)
With the current state of survivor efficiency in Dead by Daylight being greater at a baseline than it has ever been before, the demand for quick and consistent chases on Clown has simultaneously never been higher. Specific emphasis is to be placed on both quick and consistent . When chases are not consistently quick , they might as well be considered inconsistently long . While in the past this was something that could be compensated for by shorter hook times and stronger generator slowdown/regression perks, nowadays Clown just doesn’t have the macro tools to reestablish momentum against coordinated survivors assuming Clown has a bad chase – even if the reason for that bad chase was something outside of Clown’s control. It is for this reason that I argue at the moment, on Clown , “ Assurance is Premium .”
This is where Genetic Limits provides the most value. When Clown has Genetic Limits, one knows if they commit to a survivor after injury that said survivor won’t have any exhaustion perks to rely on and unknowingly extend the chase. That 8 second exhaustion timer is pretty long when the Clown player is skilled and, through practice, is able to chain tiles with strong momentum. Remember that exhaustion timers only count down when a survivor is not actively running. Further, the use of double-bottle combos prevents the use of check spots for reducing that timer. The only opportunity a survivor might have to wait out Genetic Limits is during a bottle reload. However, that’s also the same time period that a survivor should be using to leave the tile and, thus, keep the exhaustion timer paused .
Probably the most impactful use of Genetic Limits comes from denying survivors from using Dead Hard . Assuming Clown maintains strong momentum, there should be little opportunity for a survivor to remove that exhaustion timer and use Dead Hard to create a three-health-state chase. Due to how Dead Hard functions, this means that a Clown player can use Genetic Limits as part of their game sense to accurately read and gauge how much time is needed to down a survivor through two health states in order to determine if a chase is worth the effort.
Let me be explicitly clear when I say that the strength of this guarantee cannot be overstated. A single use of Dead Hard can both create a lose/lose situation for Clown in chase in addition to providing the other survivors enough time they need on a macro level to close out the rest of their objectives. I have lost entire matches because ONE survivor used Dead Hard ONE time and the rest of their team capitalized off of the time it provided. Unlike other exhaustion perks, Dead Hard is reactive and not proactive . Proactive exhaustion perks ( Sprint Burst , Lithe , Balanced Landing , Smash Hit ) are not as punishing by comparison from survivors since Clown is typically not giving up anywhere near as much time or resources before they come into play. As a result, Clown can simply choose to break the chase much earlier and look to find someone else.
Speaking of other exhaustion perks, Genetic Limits is similarly also very rewarding assuming one can injure a survivor before they have a chance to use them. Balanced Landing , in particular, is countered probably the worst by Genetic Limits given that survivors are forcibly staggered when dropping from heights in addition to not gaining the speed.
Dramaturgy is another fascinating exhaustion perk because it could be considered hit or miss as to whether or not it is or isn’t countered by Genetic Limits. While it can be used when a survivor is healthy, doing so risks the chance of exposing said survivor and giving Clown a drastically faster chase than if one didn’t use it at all. Yet at the same time, one cannot reliably use Dramaturgy in the same manner while injured unless Clown either falls behind in momentum or decides to break chase. More often than not, though, survivors are likely still going to get their best outcome of a decent speed boost while healthy to help them reach a window or pallet and no downsides.
It is possible to counter a survivor with Sprint Burst using Genetic Limits, but in most situations your ability to do so is left up to the discretion of the survivors. Unless they’re immersing with Sprint Burst and you happen to hit them before they start running, countering Sprint Burst with Genetic Limits is unlikely to happen without the combination of other anti-exhaustion perks (such as Fearmonger ).
Two more survivor perks which are surprisingly countered by Genetic Limits are Iron Will and Background Player . Because Iron Will does not mute survivor audio whenever an injured survivor is exhausted, Clown is able to track survivors easier when committing to two-health-state chases at high wall tiles. Against Background Player, if Clown can catch said survivors out of position, all he needs to do is injure them first and afterward zone with pink bottles in order to prevent them from getting either pallet or flashbang saves. Further, Clown can use that injury on the altruistic survivor after hooking their teammate in order to create better macro pressure.
Looking at perks and build synergies, I would personally suggest that Genetic Limits be run alongside at least one other chase perk at the bare minimum – preferably one which directly increases lethality (My #1 Suggestion is Coup de Grace ) and doesn’t, at best, only provide information (such as Zanshin Tactics ). If committing to an unconventional chase build with multiple chase perks and zero generator regression or slowdown, chase information perks like Zanshin Tactics are very, very good. However, when it comes to meta loadouts, there just isn’t the opportunity cost via perk slots for a perk like Zanshin Tactics to be run alongside Genetic Limits without sacrificing lethality in order to still maintain that regression/slowdown. Opting for lethality over information means that if one can compensate for that lack of information using either prior game knowledge or good judgement, one has a greater ceiling for potential performance. In turn, one will have greater agency than they would before .
The reason why I believe Coup de Grace is probably the best choice to pair with Genetic Limits is that the benefits mid-chase are two-fold. Either Coup de Grace can help Clown get an injury for Genetic Limits right before an exhaustion perk would otherwise come into play (Lithe/Balanced Landing), or it can help Clown get a down after the fact by allowing him to chain and play difficult tiles significantly better. Genetic Limits also supports Coup de Grace when chasing injured survivors with Dead Hard since Dead Hard is a natural counter to Coup de Grace due to its predictability. Removing that protection from Coup de Grace with Genetic Limits not only saves you Coup stacks when lunging against survivors with Dead Hard, but it also saves significant amounts of time.
Forced Penance (FP)
This perk might not seem valuable to some due to the means by which the effect is triggered. A protection hit is a specific type of successful attack that is not often recognized by someone playing killer unless they are actively looking for it. It is not nearly as prevalent at lower levels of gameplay since survivors of this caliber do not exhibit as much teamwork to make these protection hits happen. Although it has a strict condition, what makes Forced Penance so surprisingly strong is the actual duration of the Broken status effect and the influence it has on the match once it comes into play. Locking survivors at the injured status drastically increases your lethality during follow-up chases and enables many different forms of snowballing potential that otherwise wouldn’t be possible with Franklin’s Demise or Sloppy Butcher . Unlike either of these two perks, there is zero possibility of a survivor inflicted with Forced Penance ever becoming healthy again until after the perk expires. This is a level of assurance that is unrivaled in its category.
Forced Penance excels the most when the survivors who take protection hits do so without calculated purpose. Many survivors will position themselves for a protection hit both early and at an inopportune time under the presumption that they can undo your pressure once they run away. With Forced Penance, these survivors are unable to undo that pressure by any means for a colossal 80 seconds . That is almost the equivalent of an entire generator without any perks, toolboxes, or skill checks. It is also 1 and 1/3rd of a hook state for a survivor hanging on a hook. Further, survivors inflicted with Forced Penance still have to spend the time healing once that 80-second Broken timer expires.
In order to engineer situations where you get the most value out of Forced Penance as Clown, it is highly recommended that you tunnel extremely aggressively and put relative pressure around any hooked survivor. Survivors that bodyblock for whoever you are tunneling will be inflicted with Forced Penance. If you manage to hit a survivor that is unhooking another survivor as they are coming off of a hook, that is also considered a protection hit and will leave the unhooking survivor Broken for 80 seconds. You can also inflict Forced Penance on two unhooking survivors if they are in relative proximity and then tunnel the unhooked survivor afterward. Forced Penance essentially rewards extremely aggressive gameplay on Clown because he has the chase tools to get hits in relatively quickly if he plays well. Survivors become unable to work on generators without risk of quickly going down if they are located afterward at unsafe areas of the map. They also can’t attempt a hook save while Clown is camping due to the duration of how long they are Broken.
It should also be said that unlike a lot of other perks, you aren’t negatively impacted nearly as badly as Clown if Forced Penance doesn’t come into play from survivors bodyblocking for the tunneled survivor. If survivors purposefully avoid bodyblocking for a tunneled survivor because they don’t want to get inflicted with Forced Penance, then your job as the killer becomes automatically easier due to the lack of interference from the rest of their team. Without anyone to slow you down or prevent you from tunneling, downing a survivor shortly after they are unhooked becomes much more practical. This makes Forced Penance stronger as a perk slot even in the situations where it isn’t being triggered. In the same way that generator slowdown/regression perks are run under the expectation that survivors are going to repair generators quickly, you don’t suddenly decide to take those perks off because you have a couple of matches where the survivors aren’t being efficient. Forced Penance validates itself in this same manner, as it is but one perk slot that stands on its own to counter survivor coordination when it is needed the most.
Save the Best for Last (STBFL)
This perk is highly regarded by others as Clown’s best perk for directly increasing his lethality. To some degree, this is actually true. When you are able to get your stacks on this perk up very high (between 6-8), it allows you to end your chases faster in a way that can only be matched by running either an exposed perk or Clown’s Pinkie Finger add-on. Unlike either an exposed perk or the Pinkie Finger add-on, however, Save the Best for Last is not contingent on any other factor beyond hitting survivors that are not the obsession. Exposed perks are usually dependent on a certain condition to become active. Many of these conditions are also dependent on some form of survivor interaction that, as Clown, is directly beyond your control. This makes STBFL much more flexible and consistent for ramping up value over the course of a match when compared to those alternatives – even if the alternatives have the potential to be stronger. As long as you aren’t hitting the obsession , you can count on STBFL to eventually influence your chases in a very noticeable and positive way.
However, before we talk about the influence that the obsession has on both gaining and losing value out of this perk, it’s important to discuss stack acquisition from non-obsession survivors. Every time you hit a non-obsession target, you gain 1 stack of STBFL. This means that you should typically be gaining 2 stacks of STBFL every time you hook one non-obsession survivor. Sometimes , you may come across situations where you gain 3 stacks in between each hook. These situations should not be seen as the baseline. In order for them to take place, that typically means you were either in proximity to an unhook as it happened or you ate a Dead Hard from a survivor in chase before you downed them. Assuming you only gain 2 stacks of STBFL in chase, this means that you won’t hit 6-8 stacks until you have 3-4 consecutive hooks on non-obsession targets without any obsession interaction.
By the time you’re hitting 3-4 hooks against gen-efficient survivors (people who know how to properly split on generators – no toolbox or perks required), there’s a very good chance that they should also be done with anywhere from 2-3 generators. Not only does this level of gen-efficiency mean that you can’t afford to spread your hooks, but it also means that the match is going to be near halfway over by the time you’re done ramping up STBFL stacks. At this point, your priority needs to be using those high stacks of STBFL in order to sacrifice a survivor that you already previously hooked to get those stacks to begin with. 6-8 stacks of STBFL is incredibly powerful, but its capability has limits toward ending your chases fast enough before all the generators are completed – especially when you factor in Clown’s poor map mobility. You may sometimes find gen-efficient survivors leaving you questioning if you wouldn’t have performed better with another gen slowdown perk instead of STBFL. After all, dedicating a perk slot toward ramping up STBFL – just like any other perk – has its own opportunity cost associated with it.
Now that we’ve talked about Save the Best for Last stack acquisition, we can start to analyze the role that the obsession plays in stripping this perk of any significant value. Unlike hits on non-obsession targets, hitting the obsession directly weakens the strength of this perk by taking stacks away from you. While a single health state on a non-obsession target gives you 1 stack, hitting the obsession takes away 2. This means that with every health state you take from the obsession , you’re going to need to effectively hook a non-obsession survivor at least once in order to simply recoup that lost value. With how fast generators have the potential to be completed, you thus can’t really afford to interact with the obsession at all once you start gaining stacks. Hooking the obsession twice – assuming you hit them twice per chase – is enough lost stacks (-8) to equal the maximum threshold of the perk. If the obsession has Dead Hard and you happen to hit them while it is active, you will lose 6 stacks of STBFL in a single chase should you decide to commit toward the down. However, if you decide not to commit toward the down , then you have essentially thrown away 4 stacks for very little pressure on one survivor.
If you are consistently finding yourself at any point in the game unable to conveniently interact with anyone besides the obsession, STBFL is going to be both ineffective in chase and harmful toward winning the game due to the wasted opportunity cost. Further, smart survivors who happen to be the obsession will recognize that you have STBFL and will insert themselves into your chases with non-obsession survivors. This can force you to lose stacks if you have to hit the obsession. You also likely can’t afford to change targets onto the obsession, as hook pressure on a first-hook obsession during the midpoint of a match is hardly going to do much to slow the game down comparatively. Doing so would also force you to lose even more stacks of STBFL.
Some might consider that either needing or choosing to chase the obsession at the start of the match is at least one scenario where putting pressure on the obsession isn’t harmful toward STBFL’s value. Since you won’t have any stacks at the beginning of the game, it’s not like hitting or downing the obsession actively takes anything away from you that you previously had to earn. While this is true, what it doesn’t take into account is that the time you spent chasing the obsession could have been spent chasing a non-obsession survivor, instead. The flow of the game isn’t going to slow down because you chose to chase the obsession. If you choose to chase the obsession until he is downed at the beginning of the match, you will only be gaining 1 hook along with 0 stacks of STBFL.
Hooking the obsession early may remove some threat that they may have when it comes to interfering with your chases against non-obsession survivors. However, it also stalls your ramp-up of STBFL stacks in addition to creating zero active pressure on those non-obsession survivors themselves . While you spend time chasing the obsession at the start of the match with STBFL and earning 0 stacks, the non-obsession survivors are all doing generators without getting hooked or injured. By the time you then start chasing the non-obsession survivors to start earning your first stacks, against gen-efficient survivors, you’re likely going to be down at least 1 generator with more on the way. At that point, if you’re not able to play near perfectly in chase yourself – say you make a couple of mistakes – there’s a good chance you may struggle to get a high STBFL count before most of the generators are completed. Further, it truly begs the question how much STBFL is really helping you win the match (if at all) in this type of situation.
Worst case scenario, if you are able to get around 5-6 stacks of STBFL, you at least possess the ability to facecamp a hook and down a survivor using M1s before the unhook is completed without having to grab them. Other survivors may attempt to bodyblock while you are in the middle of your M1 recovery animation. Due to your high STBFL stacks, even if this happens, your recovery animation should be fast enough to a point where you can down either 1-2 survivors before you need to consider re-hooking anybody. Although the obsession might try to be the one bodyblocking, assuming you already have around 5-6 stacks, your recovery animation should still be fast enough to down either the obsession, a different survivor, or possibly both.
Using STBFL in this way is not something you typically want to do. There are only two situations where facecamping is strategically viable. Either, 1. You are confirming the approaching next hook state on an already hooked survivor due to the non-hooked survivors voluntarily leaving the hooked survivor to hang in order to get more generator progress. Or, 2. Survivors have done all the generators (post-gen phase) and the only smart choice you can really make is to camp a hook. Whenever facecamping has to take place during the post-gen phase, it’s usually the result of you, as Clown, lacking enough lethality during the match to get more kills before the generators were completed. While STBFL might enable you to handle these situations where you are playing from behind, depending on the frequency that this is taking place in your matches, it might suggest that using a different perk which would allow you to either stall the match out for longer or create more pressure on the survivors in a different way would be more effective for winning your matches.
Play With Your Food (PWYF)
Dynamic haste effects are almost always going to be really strong on Clown due to the impact they can have on reducing your chase times. In a lot of situations, a survivor being able to make it to a window or pallet can be the difference in that particular chase lasting 20-30 seconds longer. This is pretty significant, as it essentially translates into a downside that costs you 25-30% generator progress per generator . When playing Clown, one cannot afford to have these circumstances present themselves frequently – especially during the early game. I put emphasis on per generator for that reason: if this happens, this is likely going to be the cause of why one loses two generators during a single chase. A 20-30 second extension due to a survivor making it to a pallet or window compounded with pre-run distance at the start of that chase – on top of every other action afterward (picking up the survivor, carrying them, hooking them) – basically guarantees those generators get completed due to how much influence they have altogether on making your chases last too long.
Play With Your Food , in particular, is very excellent at handling this issue due to its flexible persistence. All a Clown player has to do is simply initiate and then end a chase with the Obsession in order to gain 1 stack of the perk. After this happens, the Haste from Play With Your Food will not expire once any period of time has elapsed. This means that one can freely move across the map more efficiently as well as choose their next chase target more wisely. Further, the decision to chase any non-obsession survivor becomes more reasonable on account of that extra haste. Another really important detail is that Clown does not lose stacks of Play With Your Food upon throwing either of his bottles or reloading.
Actually gaining a stack of Play With Your Food from a nearby obsession is the easiest part about using the perk. You start a chase with the obsession until you hear the chase music. Once you hear the music, you want to immediately look away and move in the opposite direction. The most complicated aspect of utilizing Play With Your Food is engineering situations where the obsession will be nearby so that you can gain a stack of Play With Your Food in an efficient manner . You do not want to be going out of your way looking for the obsession just so that you can gain a stack. Clown does not have the map mobility to even pressure his generators – let alone go on side quests to get haste buffs.
Almost every stack of Play With Your Food that one gains should come complimentary as a reward for what one was originally intending to do to begin with. For example:
- Pressuring a generator being repaired by two survivors – one Obsession, one-non Obsession. When you push both off the generator, you initiate chase with both survivors simultaneously. Actively engage with the non-obsession and you will gain a stack of Play With Your Food mid-chase.
- Returning to a hooked non-Obsession survivor who is about to get unhooked by the Obsession. Tunneling the now-unhooked non-Obsession survivor will result in you gaining a stack of Play With Your Food mid-chase since you can initiate a chase with the Obsession after they finish unhooking someone else.
- Stumbling across the Obsession while intending to go from Point A to Point B without having to go out of your way to initiate a chase for a Play With Your Food Stack.
Countering Play With Your Food from the perspective of the survivors is actually very difficult. Survivors as a whole either have to bewilder Clown into missing his attacks or the Obsession can’t go anywhere near Clown at any point during the game. The latter might seem reasonable, but an Obsession who refuses to go near the killer is essentially one less player who will be attempting unhooks or bodyblocking for the non-Obsession teammate that Clown decides to tunnel. This puts more impetus on the other non-Obsession survivors to get involved protecting their tunneled teammate, which means now there are less people repairing generators as this happens.
In order to play into this strength further, I highly recommend running Play With Your Food alongside Save the Best for Last – even after the nerf. The recovery time is still very quick, and you’ll now be able to punish the Obsession throughout the match no matter what they decide to do. If they put themselves near you, you’ll gain Play With Your Food; if they try to avoid you, you’ll max out your stacks of Save the Best for Last from all of their non-Obsession teammates. Either way, you’ll have much greater lethality for killing their team with any counterplay to the combo being impractical.
Another suggestion on perk synergy is to take Coup de Grace . Coup de Grace extends your lunging duration – meaning that you move at a faster speed for a longer period of time . The speed at which one lunges is modified by Haste effects. If you have stacks of Play With Your Food, you will not only lunge faster but lunge farther. Similarly, this distance and speed can also be extended by using Clown’s yellow bottles in tandem with Play With Your Food.
Batteries Included (BI)
For the longest time, Clown has not had access to flexible and dynamic Haste effects beyond what could be provided by his own bottles. Perks like Hex: Devour Hope and Hex: No One Escapes Death were too circumstantial and limited with respect to catching survivors while the Haste buffs were still active. Play With Your Food has always been an option, but its synergy with Save the Best for Last was never realized for quite some time. Even then, Play With Your Food has somewhat of a requirement associated with gaining stacks as well as maintaining them. This gives Batteries Included (BI)  a unique advantage over other Haste perks, as it will always remain active until a match ends assuming that the activating condition is fulfilled.
The best way to understand how to make the most of BI is by breaking down what it actually does. Once a generator is completed, a horizontal and vertical sphere with a 12 meter radius (24 meter diameter) is formed around that completed generator. If you walk into that sphere, you will gain 5% Haste until 5 seconds have transpired after leaving the sphere. The time you spend within the sphere itself does not matter – merely grazing the edge is enough to activate the perk and give you the minimum of 5% haste for that 5 second duration. Which, in a way, makes Batteries Included extremely similar to Clown’s yellow bottle. Except unlike the yellow bottle, BI does not provide the haste benefit to survivors within the radius. The advantage of BI is purely one-sided, making it tactfully superior in that regard. Even so, probably the best aspect of BI is the fact that one can compound its effects with Clown’s yellow bottle for an even greater Haste bonus than what would be achieved by either individually.
How one should use Batteries Included in chase is by viewing the center of a completed generator as the center of where one would have thrown a yellow bottle. Whenever you’re pathing by any generator that is fully repaired, you should be intending to efficiently cross through the radius of those generators in order to gain BI’s effect. This is relevant when you’re either chaining between multiple tiles or if you’re playing one specific tile that is positioned in a way for you to take advantage of that generator’s location. In the latter situation, BI can essentially function as a third bottle with how it can be used alongside Clown’s power.
On this point, probably the most impactful benefit of Batteries Included is that it enables one to shut down certain independent Map-specific tiles that are otherwise impossible to deal with even when using Coup de Grace . This could be because these tiles are either inter-connected to other other strong tiles, possess strong pallets, windows, or all of the above. Coup de Grace can only go so far alongside both Clown’s pink + yellow bottles – and the 5% haste from BI makes up the difference necessary.
Here are some examples of Map-specific tiles where Batteries Included would be extremely valuable:
Dead Dawg Saloon
Survivors typically like to do the generator located at the Saloon very early. This is because it’s a very safe generator to complete on account of how hard it is for Clown to directly pressure it. Assuming this happens, this main building now becomes much less safer for the survivors since the range of the generator extends across almost the entire Saloon itself. The upstairs window vault at the back as well as the downstairs window vault at the front become more susceptible to pink bottle placements since you are now more likely to have the movement speed to align yourself for the proper angles. As a result, it becomes more practical to catch survivors before they can use either of these resources to displace you between them.
Garden of Joy
This house can be approached from multiple angles – enabling survivors to play it in a variety of troublesome ways that are extremely hard for Clown to counter. Thankfully, there is a guaranteed generator spawn on the second floor that survivors also like to complete very early (just like with Dead Dawg Saloon) and makes the whole structure much more unsafe for survivors to utilize. Once this generator is completed, the radius usually covers the entrance of the front door <-> all the way to the end of the back exit. Depending on how much distance is between Clown and a survivor whenever they choose to go here, the existence of Batteries Included can enable Clown to catch survivors before they have a chance to either vault one of these key windows or throw down its key pallets.
Haddonfield
Similar to Garden of Joy, the house on this map can be approached from multiple angles and possesses multiple strong windows. However, unlike Garden of Joy, the main building does not have any pallets. Survivors typically like to do this generator early since it makes this strong structure a very valuable time waster for Clown if he has to chase survivors there. With Batteries Included, the radius of this generator extends down to the bottom floor of the front door all the way to the back exit. It could even potentially go as far as to cover the outside of the house on the right. Thus, safely looping this main building as a survivor becomes a lot less practical.
Racoon Police Department East
This corner is probably the strongest location on the whole map. The pallet in the room ahead is not only virtually impossible to play once pre-dropped, but it’s extremely hard to play even without being pre-dropped due to the pallet loop’s shape and size. Whenever a survivor takes Clown here, without Batteries Included, one can be expected to add a minimum of 20-25+ seconds onto that specific chase depending on how the survivor chooses to play it. If one brought Batteries Included, it now becomes more likely to catch a survivor before they make it back to the pallet assuming Clown initially has pallet possession. This is because the radius of the completed generator above extends down to cover the pallet loop below.
It is also important to mention that the placement/proximity of other nearby generators from this loop can provide an opportunity to extend the lingering duration of Batteries Included even further as you pass by more than one completed generator. This makes it harder for survivors to safely chain tiles and can result in you reaching survivors while they are still out in the open before they can make it to another window or pallet.
Ormond Resort
This is a pretty great main building when it comes to getting value out of Batteries Included since the radius extends out by the front door. Unless you’re entering this building from the back, you should be able to maintain the haste buff of Batteries Included as you chase a survivor throughout the main building until you reach either the upstairs windows or pallets. Survivors also like to do this generator early, so you should be able to get both consistent and persistent value out of this perk whenever you end up on this map.
Badham Preschool
Chases throughout the preschool are typically very obnoxious since it’s very difficult to angle double-bottle combos around it and survivors usually will not take a chase to this location unless they have a fair amount of prior distance between you and them as it is. Batteries Included makes this area a lot more manageable since the radius on the bottom floor extends upward outside the school on the right side of the building. This is also the same direction that survivors will usually loop when it comes to the outside of the main building due to the white fence being inconvenient for Clown to navigate.
Mother’s Dwelling
This is another main building that can be played multiple ways and has multiple strong windows and pallets. The location of the generator means that its radius once it is completed should extend across most of the building on both the top and bottom floor. Only the backside of the main building is likely to not be covered under the radius. However, the duration should linger long enough for it to matter assuming you take your chase starting from the frontside of the building, first.
Gideon Meat Plant
Since this is a vertical map, the potential value of Batteries Included is much higher here than on other maps by comparison. There are a variety of pallet loops on this map that become much easier to manage (even when they are pre-dropped) as a result of a generator on the opposite floor being previously completed. These two are the most notable.
—————————————————————————————
Another important fact about Batteries Included is that once five generators are repaired, all seven generator spawns receive the effect of Batteries Included. This means that during the endgame phase there are seven different locations where you can potentially utilize Batteries Included in order to convert any final pressure before the match is finished.
Although everything I’ve said so far makes Batteries Included sound very, very good, there are still some contingent downsides that one ought to take into account.  Most notably: survivors themselves have full control and agency over which generators are completed first, when this happens, and where they want to lead their chases. Some survivor teams may unintentionally counter Batteries Included by leaving one of these key generators uncompleted until the late game – meaning that you might be forced to take chases at these locations throughout the match without gaining value from the perk. Until there are few options left, survivors might also choose by unrelated circumstances to take their chases to areas away from completed generators simply due to those other areas either being stronger tiles or having pallets that have yet to be used.
Beyond that, Batteries Included is a fantastic chase perk and one that a Clown player could reliably count upon in their matches due to the synergy that it has with other chase perks (like Coup de Grace ) as well as Clown’s power. Other than Coup de Grace, one could also run Batteries Included alongside either Rapid Brutality or Play With Your Food for even greater haste in chase that survivors you go against might not expect you to have.
Unrelenting
On initial glance, this perk might seem like it does not have any legitimate benefit. This is because people typically want to ensure they land their basic attacks to begin with. A perk which only helps when one misses their basic attacks is, thus, unattractive when one’s perspective is narrowed to believe that all missed basic attacks are avoidable. While this can technically be true, it is only true insofar as one is willing to play without taking any risks. If you are unwilling to take risks, your overall chase times will be longer as a result.
Distance and efficient movement make up a significant role with respect to catching survivors at a tile before they make it to a window or a pallet. At higher levels of play, these elements become so tight that Clown can become reliant on taking calculated risks by trying to catch survivors during an animation lock – e.g. throwing down a pallet, vaulting a pallet, or vaulting a window. Survivors who anticipate that this is what Clown is intending to do can pretend to do said action while running in a different direction in order to avoid Clown’s lunge. If the survivor guesses correctly, the long missed recovery time on a basic attack enables said survivor to either gain enough distance at that individual tile or chain to a new one altogether. This can be rather punishing for Clown, and depending on the circumstances, can enable generators to become completed before that chase is over – hence why some people opt not to take that risk.
Where Unrelenting comes into play is that these same calculated risks , instead, become calculated decisions . The choice to lunge is no longer a risk since the faster missed recovery time from Unrelenting means that there is zero advantage granted to a survivor for avoiding your attack. They will not make it to that window or that pallet. It is now a decision that is still calculated since one is acting with the knowledge that no matter what the survivor does, Unrelenting means that, at best, a survivor can extend their chase time by only 3-5 seconds.
How Unrelenting essentially works in practice is that due to having a shorter missed recovery time, you end up accelerating back to your normal movement speed even faster . This basically translates as to say that Unrelenting gives you free meters of distance for closing in on survivors following a missed basic attack. Survivors are not able to reach a window or pallet safely on account of Unrelenting increasing the Killer back to their regular movement speed so quickly. In essence, this is to say that Unrelenting removes a major part of survivor skill expression . One cannot counter a perk or strategy if, at base, they are physically lacking in the agency to do so.

In this particular chase, I am currently out of bottles and am trying to reach Nea before she makes it to a window or a pallet. Due to the prior distance between us, I am unable to catch her before she would have made it to that pallet – hence my choice to rotate. Before I am fully around the wall, I hear Nea drop said pallet, which is what prompted my decision to swing. This is because the sound que denoted she initiated an animation-lock. My intention was to catch her either while she was still in the middle of an animation-lock from dropping the pallet or as she was sliding over it.
However, Nea also understood that this is what I was going to do and she chose to rotate around the wall instead of vaulting the pallet. If I didn’t have Unrelenting during this chase, I wouldn’t have caught her before she made it back to the pallet. I then would’ve had to reload my bottles and attempt to catch her with a yellow-pink combo while also moving in a way to actively prevent her from leaving that tile to begin with – which could have extended this chase time anywhere from 15-20+ seconds at a minimum.
Unrelenting , during this chase, essentially rewarded me for getting outplayed by the survivor . There aren’t many (if any) perks that let you do this so blatantly. When one also considers that being without bottles is supposed to be Clown’s weakest point in chase to begin with, it makes the influence that it had on this chase even more valuable with respect to the alternative of playing without it.
Unrelenting Killer Shack Example - Video Link
During this chase, I had a Vitto that was taking me to Killer Shack very early into a match. I end up missing my pink bottle and, as a result, have to rely on just my yellow bottle for the rotation. As we are moving around the window-side of the Killer Shack, Vitto attempts to bait me into swinging at him by pretending to vault the window. This is a very typical mind-game at Killer Shack that survivors will do, as without Unrelenting I wouldn’t have caught him before he made it back to the pallet. One could almost guarantee that Vitto wasn’t expecting me to have Unrelenting – as why should he? It is not only an extremely uncommon perk, but one that people do not view as having this potential.
For posterity, I will acknowledge that I reacted slightly late when it came to pre-emptively lunging to counter the potential window vault. However, the fact that I could still catch Vitto afterward still showcases the very same point – Unrelenting removes risk by rewarding those who utilize missed lunges intentionally as part of a slightly bigger picture .
I also want to briefly draw attention to how early into the match this particular chase took place. There are still five uncompleted generators remaining, which means that Coup de Grace couldn’t have fulfilled a similar role for Unrelenting with respect to this scenario. The fact that Unrelenting is available immediately after a match starts and isn’t limited in how many times it can gain value is really significant with regard to establishing early pressure.
Being able to save 15-20+ seconds so early into a match can translate into becoming your literal game-winning condition with how much stress it can impose on the other survivors to have to play around it. Not only will they be behind on generator progress, but the foreboding threat of you tunneling out that first-hooked survivor very early could potentially create an unwinnable scenario afterward where the remaining survivors cannot keep up with Clown as a roaming threat.

What I love about this example is that it perfectly encapsulates everything I’ve mentioned thus far with regard to how this perk is meant to be used. My yellow and pink bottles were purposefully placed to where I could hit Dwight no matter what he did. If he rotated around the pallet loop, I would easily catch him because of my yellow and pink bottles. However, vaulting the pallet loop had no advantage, either, as Unrelenting meant that I could catch him before he made it to another window or pallet. All of this was by intentional design. I want to be clear when I say this is a tertiary benefit , as those missed swings following the first shouldn’t have happened. Regardless, this fact about Unrelenting should be mentioned on account of how differently the outcome would have been if I couldn’t recover fast enough following that second missed swing.
Probably the most complicated aspect of running Unrelenting is figuring out how to viably place it within a perk build. If one opts to run two generator slowdown/regression perks, this would mean that taking Unrelenting would leave only one other perk slot for any type of flexibility. Assuming Coup de Grace is also chosen, that flexibility is virtually non-existent. However, Unrelenting is a wonderful compliment for Coup de Grace since it essentially extends the range by which Unrelenting can be used for this purpose. By running both perks together, one can remove even more agency from survivors mid-chase since not only will one’s lunges go farther, but missing those lunges either bears zero consequence or rewards one for simply making the effort.
I would personally not recommend running Unrelenting without Coup de Grace unless you simply don’t like Coup de Grace in general or purposefully want to play without it. The latter is going to have a much more consistent and greater impact on your chases. This is not to discount anything I’ve said thus far with regard to Unrelenting itself. Coup de Grace is just ubiquitously better in almost every way. Probably the only main advantage that Unrelenting has over Coup de Grace is that one can utilize it during the very beginning of a match. Both are great chase perks; one is just better .
If you’re not interested in meta viability or optimal perk build design, I will at least say that Unrelenting is a very independent perk with zero reliance on any other perk or add-on to function properly. This means that you could fit Unrelenting into probably any perk build that has at least one slot for flexibility. I want to be clear and re-emphasize that one is not required to take Coup de Grace alongside Unrelenting in order to gain effective Unrelenting value. In fact, every chase example shown within this guide demonstrated value from only Unrelenting and Unrelenting alone .
Grim Embrace
This most recent patch has turned Grim Embrace from a useless and ineffectual generator blocking perk into one of the best generator blocking perks in the entire game. Being able to block all generators is a level of indirect map pressure that Clown desperately needs due to his poor map mobility. By itself, the value is still somewhat limited until the fourth survivor is hooked for the first time. However, Grim Embrace truly shines after every first hook on each survivor once you start running other generator regression or blocking perks alongside it. For this reason, it’s important to spend the most time looking at its two best perk synergies: Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance and Dead Man’s Switch .
What allows these three perks to work so well together is that they all have global presence. Clown is, thus, not physically required to be at a generator’s location in order for these perks to provide value or interact together. Survivors not only have to get off of a generator early in order to prevent Pain Resonance from applying Dead Man’s Switch , but they also are forced to stay off the generator for that same reason due to Grim Embrace . Yet by doing so, they allow that generator to passively regress following Pain Resonance’s -20% . This creates a really problematic scenario for survivors – especially if you are playing against either solo-queue or uncoordinated teams.
While some may say that Grim Embrace doesn’t promote camping or tunneling, this is only true if considered by itself . When Grim Embrace is combined with Pain Resonance and Dead Man’s Switch , it’s a lot harder for survivor teammates to support one another since hook trades will convert into -20% progress on the most repaired generator right before every generator gets blocked, afterward. Clown can then seek to tunnel out that recently unhooked survivor knowing it is impossible for their teammates to be repairing generators in the background and someone else has to come unhook the person who traded.
In fact, the real potential of Grim Embrace presents itself assuming you manage to sacrifice one survivor rather early. If one survivor is already dead before you hook the fourth survivor for the first time, the extended generator-blocking on every generator can likely be enough pressure for you to snowball the rest of the match. Any chase that you take will be without the consequence of lost generator progress and that survivor you hook is guaranteed to be at a minimum on their second hook stage. If survivors group up to heal while all of the generators are still blocked, they risk getting slugged and losing outright. One can somewhat engineer this possibility with greater frequency by ensuring that the Obsession is not the last survivor to be hooked for the first time since their aura will be visible after that fourth hook.
Brutal Strength (Brutal)
This is a very common perk that people like to run on Clown due to their propensity to kick a lot of pallets. With how Clown’s power is designed for countering survivors even around pre-dropped pallets, I really don’t see the value in this perk for players that are skilled at executing double-bottle combos. The time you will spend kicking pallets – even with the increased speed – is usually still not going to be enough to hit survivors that are running to another tile before they reach an area of safety. While you may be able to remove some pallets at different areas of the map in order to create dead zones faster, it is likely that these pallet loops are just as vulnerable even with the pallet already dropped and unbroken due to tile-slicing .
If you aren’t able to use the increased pallet-kicking speed from Brutal Strength to hit a survivor before they are able to reach a safe area at another tile, then it is effectually useless at decreasing your chase time. You are going to go through the same motions of kicking more and more pallets as you would without the perk earning your hooks any faster to show for it. At that point, it is essentially just cosmetic and for flair rather than being actually practical.
As far as kicking generators goes, even with your increased kicking speed, survivors who run directly away from the tile as you run up to kick the generator are still going to create enough distance to reach an area of safety. When playing as Clown, the biggest problem with kicking generators that survivors are actively working on has always been that survivors are going to use that time to create a lot of distance. Brutal Strength doesn’t change that. Without something direct like Pop Goes the Weasel, Call of Brine, or Overcharge , you can’t really afford in a lot of situations to kick a generator that a survivor is working on because the base generator regression won’t create enough pressure. The smartest decision is usually always to opt for chasing a survivor instead of kicking a generator due to how much faster it enables you to down them and get another hook. Whenever there is any downtime in between hooking survivors, the increased kicking speed from Brutal Strength is also going to do hardly anything at all. Further, quite literally any other hook-dependent gen regression/slowdown perk would be more valuable in that situation, instead.
Personally, outside of niche experimentation, I do not recommend anyone run Brutal Strength on Clown with any serious consideration. Even for those that are still learning Clown and are finding themselves kicking a lot of pallets, I would still suggest you run anything else that has a good chance of being more practical. I also partially believe that for Clown, specifically, Brutal Strength hinders your growth and understanding of how to execute double-bottle combos at different tiles by encouraging you to immediately default toward kicking a dropped pallet whenever you feel any adversity. In order to actually learn for yourself how to properly use both of Clown’s bottles together around a pre-dropped pallet, you have to have the courage when the time comes to not kick the pallet .
Corrupt Intervention (CI)
After the nerfs, I think it’s fair to say that much of the value from this perk is misunderstood by the majority of players. Even I, myself, initially considered this perk to be mediocre on account of how volatile its overall duration can be. Depending on how long it takes for you to get your first down, this perk can be active from anywhere in between 20-60 seconds or perhaps even longer . For this reason, running a different perk in its stead can seem more attractive for players who find themselves catching survivors out early and downing them quickly. The problem with this line of thinking is that it disregards other aspects of the early game that are impacted by Corrupt Intervention and are also arguably more prevalent.
Rather than look at the strength of Corrupt Intervention based on how long you can keep generators blocked overall, one should view it from the perspective of which generators are blocked from the very beginning . Almost every survivor is virtually guaranteed to spawn next to a generator. Without Corrupt Intervention, this means that survivors are immediately able to start gaining progress on their objective at the start of every match . This is even more impactful against Clown due to his poor map mobility. Maps like Borgo, Ormond, Garden of Joy, Azarov’s Resting Place, Suffocation Pit, and RPD are good examples of where not having Corrupt Intervention can be harmful to you even if you manage to have a fast and early chase. The first survivor that you do manage to pressure is likely going to have gained some progress on a generator before you start chasing them. Assuming that you have survivors who double-up on a key generator, you could end up receiving a significant blow in the form of a broken 3-gen from the very beginning. If your 3-gen is broken very early into a match, your chances of winning as Clown are significantly diminished since it now becomes much harder to defend whichever generators that remain.
In order to utilize Corrupt Intervention most properly on Clown during the early game, one should adopt a more patient and calculated playstyle. Rather than seek out survivors to start your first chase as early as possible no matter where they are, one should instead identify the extent of the map that is filled with non-Corrupted generators and stay within that area. This zone with non-Corrupted generators is what I like to call your Non-Corrupted Web . It is essentially the only area of the map before you get your first down where survivors are capable of applying any pressure. Outside of literal competitive matches, you should not voluntarily seek out survivors who exist beyond the radius of your Non-Corrupted Web. Most survivors will try to sneak past you into the Non-Corrupted Web during the first two minutes of the game rather than wait out the full duration of Corrupt Intervention so that they can get early generator progress. These are the survivors you want to prioritize in your very first chase. Below is a basic mock-up example of what a Non-Corrupted Web could look like if you were sent to the map Suffocation Pit .
When looking at this template, what we can essentially see is that Corrupt Intervention removes any desire or need to put pressure on the top half of the map right as the match begins. Instead, we are encouraged and enabled as Clown to limit our map pressure within the area that has non-Corrupted generators. There is no concern for survivors who choose not to over-extend into our Non-Corrupted Web as it is impossible for them to otherwise gain any generator progress. This is really beneficial for Clown due to his poor map mobility since you’re not required to pressure the entirety of the map right at the very beginning.
The edge of the Non-Corrupted Web functions very similarly to tile-slicing . It denotes how far outward on the map you should and need to extend when defending the generators within your Non-Corrupted Web. Going beyond this edge in order to chase a survivor is harmful in the same way that it is harmful when a soccer goalie leaves his post; you are leaving your non-Corrupted generators wide open for other survivors to move past you and ignore your entire existence. When you do this, it’s free real estate. This is probably one of the most common reasons as to why some people who play killer lose generators very early into the game even when they’re running Corrupt Intervention – they aren’t actually identifying their Corrupted and non-Corrupted generators and applying their pressure accordingly.
Overall, I would consider Corrupt Intervention to be one of Clown’s best generator slowdown perks and a fantastic choice in just about any build. It stands on its own without any perk dependencies and helps out Clown both during the early game and on tough maps where he can’t afford to pressure the whole map right as the match starts. When utilized properly, it can give you the early game snowball that you need in order to remove a survivor quick enough from the game so that you can secure the win. I would still run it alongside another generator slowdown/regression perk, but the wonderful thing about Corrupt Intervention is that it doesn’t have any anti-synergy with any other perk in its category. Deadlock would probably be my go-to choice when selecting a secondary generator slowdown perk alongside Corrupt Intervention due to how flexible it is at snowballing whatever advantage you may have previously gained while Corrupt Intervention was active.
Dead Man’s Switch (DMS)
Following the recent rework that this perk received, it has become dramatically better at providing the necessary global presence that Clown needs in order to stop generator repairs that are beyond his map mobility. With a 50-second active timer, Dead Man’s Switch effectively slows down a single generator’s repair progress by 55 %. There is not a single means survivors have in order to remove Dead Man’s Switch early. Once it is triggered on a generator, survivors are forced to either wait it out or rotate to a different generator at a different location on the map.
One major downside this perk used to have is that it would count down from its timer immediately after a survivor is hooked. Now, Dead Man’s Switch only activates once a survivor gets off of a generator – ensuring that the perk gets close to maximum value every single time! Keep in mind, however, that if you are too skilled in your chases Dead Man’s Switch won’t overlap and provide you with simultaneous multiple activations. Then again, if that is happening, more often than not there might not be too much reason to complain.
I would consider pairing Dead Man’s Switch with Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance in order to get the most value out of both perks. Grim Embrace is also a decent choice as it also provides the same type of global presence for more generators at once. There is also the possibility that Dead Man’s Switch can now stand on its own depending on the map and tile if one were to run DMS by itself in an all chase build – such as Coup de Grace , Zanshin Tactics , Genetic Limits , and Dead Man’s Switch . Unless you’re going against super coordinated teams, it’s likely that many survivors will be caught off guard by both your micro chase pressure combined with the overwhelming solo gen-blocking duration of Dead Man’s Switch.
Deadlock (Deadlock)
Compared to other gen-blocking perks like Corrupt Intervention or Dead Man’s Switch , Deadlock used to be much more effective and consistent at both giving you information and slowing down survivor gen speeds. However, the recent nerfs to Deadlock alongside the rework of Dead Man’s Switch has made Deadlock, comparatively, the worse option. 25 seconds is really not that much generator slowdown when one considers that it also requires the loss of a different generator in order to provide this value. A perk like Dead Man’s Switch, however, can now be consistently applied for much greater generator blocking without such a demanding cost.
Probably the greatest aspect of Deadlock has to be its global presence. There isn’t any map mobility required in order for the perk to do its job. This allows you to use it in situations where you need to simultaneously pressure multiple generators on one side of the map as you’re mid-chase with a key survivor on the other. Usually, this is most prevalent when you’re chasing a survivor that has already been hooked once or twice. Assuming that a generator is completed while you’re still mid-chase, you can rely on Deadlock to block the other generator and buy you enough time to down then hook whomever it is that you’re chasing. This can afford you the necessary advantage that you need in order to close out some matches and win. Even though you should be trying to defend as many generators as possible, sometimes you need to recognize the extent of your map pressure and when you need to exchange your generators for necessary hooks. In some of these scenarios, opting to run Deadlock can be what tilts these exchanges in your favor.
Looking at downsides, probably the biggest issue Deadlock has is that it doesn’t synergize very well with most gen regression perks. Generators that are blocked by Deadlock still receive but do not lose any generator progress from down/hook-dependent regression perks like Pain Resonance , Jolt , or Eruption . Further, any generator that is blocked by Deadlock will not regress passively at all. This means perks like Call of Brine , Overcharge , or Ruin will also have zero effect as long as a generator is blocked by Deadlock. Even Pop Goes the Weasel can be crippled by Deadlock if it is triggered 10-15 seconds after hooking a survivor since you won’t have time to kick the generator before PGTW expires. As much as Deadlock can help you when you’re behind, it can also really hurt you if it activates at an inconvenient time – especially now with the reduced duration. Survivors have complete agency over wherever and whenever Deadlock takes place  – you don’t. If you’re going to run Deadlock – especially with other gen regression perks – this is something you really need to take into account.
Rancor (Rancor)
For the longest time, I never really took Rancor seriously. It reads like a gimmicky late-game perk that offers a very small early-mid game benefit at the expense of also punishing you during the early-mid game, as well. While the location reveal can be incredibly useful when it comes to immediately discerning survivor strategy, a smart obsession survivor will know that you have the perk and will adjust their playstyle accordingly. Once most of the generators are completed, many will either camp the exit gates, hide in lockers, or immerse themselves well beyond your terror radius. If you aren’t able to locate the obsession around the time the last generator is about to be completed, there is usually going to be very little chance that you will have gained enough value out of the perk if the obsession is playing properly. The location reveal is merely supplementary given that Clown’s poor map mobility usually means you won’t be able to directly act on it even if you wanted.
Perk slots are very valuable. When running Rancor, you’re essentially forfeiting the opportunity to bring a perk that either slows down generator completion speed or makes your chases end faster. It’s similar to Hex: No One Escapes Death , in a way, given that you are playing the majority of the match with close to only three active perks. While there potentially may be a really rewarding payoff near the end of the match, every stage before it is going to be significantly harder due to Rancor offering likely either little or zero value. This can result in more survivors being alive when the post-gen phase begins. Coordinated teams with enough survivors alive to support the obsession can actually prevent you from catching the obsession before they can reach the exit gates. Just because the obsession is exposed and instantly killable doesn’t mean that they are a guaranteed kill once the last generator is completed. As Clown, you have to respect a strong survivor’s ability to chain tiles, move efficiently, and whether or not they have any exhaustion perks like Lithe or Balanced Landing that they can use in clutch moments while healthy.
Even with these downsides in mind, I have recently discovered after some very focused testing that Rancor might actually be on the level of a generator regression/slowdown perk if you adopt a really aggressive playstyle. As I’ve said previously, Rancor is weakened during the post-gen phase if there are enough survivors alive to support the obsession. However, this means that the opposite is also true depending on how many survivors you can kill before that phase happens. Killing survivors reduces the number of location-reveals upon generator completion and makes tracking the obsession throughout the match much easier if you use the process of elimination. You can also supplement that process with your own knowledge of what you have seen in order to be certain of your tracking.
The most effective and aggressive method of taking a survivor out of the match is by relentless and unforgiving tunneling. When running Rancor, you want to prioritize all non-obsession survivors above every other objective – even defending your generators. Having to either kick generators or patrol the map just isn’t practical since you will usually be giving breathing room to the survivor with the most hook pressure. You don’t want to be close enough to where you’re camping, but you want to be close enough to where you can anticipate when the unhook will take place and intercept the unhooked survivor shortly after. If you remain too close, then you may delay when the unhook happens and, thus, put off when you can tunnel the unhooked survivor. This will likely result in the rest of the survivors getting free generator progress as they know they have plenty of time in between each hook state. The only time you ever want to camp a hook with Rancor is to confirm the 2nd hook state after survivors purposefully let someone sit on the hook for most of the 1st hook state’s duration. You then want to leave as soon as possible so that the survivors unhook and you can then tunnel.
What makes tunneling with Rancor so effective when compared to other playstyles is that Rancor lets you circumvent the entire gameplay loop for one survivor on the team. You can go into every match knowing that, at most , you aren’t playing for 12 hook states, you’re playing for 9 . That’s a massive deal when it comes to meeting your maximum win condition as a killer – especially when you consider that those hook states aren’t being spread around . It’s even more astonishing in the event you’re only going for three kills instead of four. In that case, you only need a total of 6 hook states in order to make getting three kills very practical.
It is because of this synergy that I would argue Rancor, individually, has the single-greatest influence on one’s kill rate assuming that one can consistently locate the obsession near the start of the post-gen phase. Even in the event where a killer has a rough match and is only able to kill one non-obsession survivor ( 25% ) by the time the last generator is about to be completed, Rancor enables the killer to have the possibility of bumping that kill rate up to 50% assuming that they can locate and hit the obsession one single time. Once that hit occurs, there is borderline zero baseline altruistic counterplay available to the other survivors, as the obsession can be mori’d on the spot. This makes actually losing a match very difficult if getting two kills is considered a draw. In order to lose a match, you would essentially have to fail at tunneling one survivor to sacrifice before all 5 generators are completed. Yet, even then, Rancor would still give you the possibility of walking away from a loss that bad with at least one kill. That’s how powerful it can really be.
In order to get the most value out of Rancor while utilizing extreme forms of tunneling, I’ve found that Deadlock has surprisingly a very powerful synergy. Gen-efficient survivors are usually going to be spread out across the map and working on multiple generators at one time. Running Deadlock lets you double-down on tunneling a survivor by granting you that extra 30-second window in between losing another generator anywhere on the map . This can be a major deal against very strong teams, as that should be enough time for you to confirm another hook state on a tunneled survivor. I have gone against teams where they hop off generators in masse because they recognized how quickly I could end my chases compared to how long Deadlock was active and they knew that whoever I was tunneling needed some bodyblocks in order to survive. Although Deadlock doesn’t synergize well with other regression/slowdown perks, it performs surprisingly well all on its own. Not only is the global presence of Deadlock a major factor, but the actual generator-blocking is a stronger counter to coordinated survivors trying to focus the final remaining generators. It also doesn’t require you to change up how you play, nor is it dependent on any contingent factors in order to get value. I can seldom imagine a better perk for pairing with it.
When putting together a template build with Rancor, I wouldn’t consider it for the position of a slowdown/regression slot unless that other slowdown/regression perk is Deadlock. If you don’t want to run Deadlock and Rancor together, I think Rancor would still fit in the template as a flex perk with any other two slowdown/regression perks of your choice. Hook-dependent instant regression perks like Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance or Pop Goes the Weasel might also work well due to the emphasis placed on tunneling assuming you can end your chases fast enough. The only perks you mostly want to avoid with Rancor are those that either require you to put pressure on the obsession early or only increase in power as you hook multiple survivors.
Eruption (Eruption)
Ever since the nerf, the value of Eruption on Clown has dropped significantly on account of it losing the incapacitation debuff for survivors. 12 seconds of aura reading might be really amazing for other killers who have strong map mobility, but the problem with Clown is that he can’t really capitalize off of this information unless one happens to be playing on a very small map. Almost all of the value Clown gains from Eruption is now just the instant regression alone. Given the work required to attain that instant regression, one almost has to wonder if it’s actually worth the effort nor the perk slot.
Eruption requires you to go out of your way to kick a generator in order to become active. This means sacrificing either more map pressure (which is already very poor to begin with) or the opportunity to initiate an early chase before a survivor is capable of gaining distance. Outside of being extremely far ahead in hooks/kills, you’re seldom ever going to find yourself in a position where you can feel comfortable kicking generators without there being a cost associated with it. There are 4 survivors and only 1 of you. As you’re spending time kicking instead of initiating chases, there are potentially up to three generators that the other survivors are collectively working on.
For Clown, this can make applying Eruption to multiple generators in between downing survivors after the cooldown expires extremely difficult. Unless you’re actively pressuring only a single area of the map (such as a 3-gen setup during the late-game), you might find that you’re only going to be able to efficiently apply Eruption to one generator in between each survivor down. Going out of your way to avoid chases so that you can apply Eruption to several generators is a losing strategy against gen-efficient survivors. By the time you finish downing one survivor after walking all over the map to kick multiple generators, gen-efficient survivors will have likely finished one of those generators in the process. The value of Eruption only comes into play once you actually down a survivor.
Theoretically, the best time to apply Eruption to generators while minimizing how long you spend outside of another chase would be either right after downing or hooking a survivor. However, Eruption’s cooldown means that this isn’t possible. If you decide to kick a nearby generator within 30 seconds of downing a survivor, it will not apply Eruption on the next survivor down to that generator. Given that you can’t afford to wait out that 30 second cooldown to apply Eruption before moving on to another area, this means that Eruption does not allow you to capitalize on the downtime immediately following you successfully ending a chase.
Some might consider that perk synergy may allow Eruption to perform a lot better. While Eruption does have some synergy with other instant regression perks, Eruption’s problem is that the new mechanic put in place to counter ‘3-genning’ limits the total number of ‘instant regression events’ that can be applied to a single generator to 8 . An ‘instant regression event’ is anything from a basic generator kick to a perk’s actual intended effect. Once this limit is reached, a generator is prevented from ever regressing for the rest of the match .
Since Eruption requires a basic generator kick, this means that Eruption counts as two instant regression events for a single application without considering any other perks. One could pair Eruption with Pop Goes the Weasel , but PGTW also has the same problem. Applying PGTW a single time on the same generator that was previously hit with Eruption once means that generator has now already experienced 4 ‘instant regression events.’
Given that perks like Pop Goes the Weasel or Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance could be combined together without fear of hitting this limit, Eruption is essentially both inferior and detrimental with respect to those other perks gaining their most value over the course of a full match. One might find themselves in situations where Eruption hardly provided any instant regression on its own while also denying one’s other instant regression perks from affecting those generators afterward.
Overall, I don’t feel 100% comfortable recommending this perk to other Clown players due to the influence of the new anti 3-gen mechanic along with the counterintuitive nature of its cooldown in applying Eruption to other generators. As a result, I don’t really see it as a perk that is going to consistently help you win your matches unless you develop some specific tailored build while bringing specific map offerings. There are definitely worse perks that you could be running, but I also think there are plenty of perks that would be much better.
Call of Brine (COB)
This perk used to have some decent potential on Clown when you could simultaneously camp both a survivor on a hook and any nearby generators. It would provide a substantial boost to passive regression that you could ensure would give value if you happened to be nearby. With the recent nerfs in the 6.7 patch, the increased passive regression has been significantly nerfed to a point where it isn’t viable at all. You would do better taking a chase perk that doesn’t influence generator repair speeds whatsoever instead of taking Call of Brine . It is now that bad.
The main problem with Call of Brine is that it only has an active duration of 60 seconds. This means that – no matter what – the maximum value you will ever gain from a single application of Call of Brine on a generator is 3.75 seconds . You gain 3.75 seconds from a whole perk slot, but also only if the survivors don’t touch it all for 60 seconds . By comparison to other slowdown or  regression perks, Call of Brine might now arguably be the worst generator perk in the entire game. Jolt gives you 7.2 seconds of regression instantly without even having to kick a generator. It can also apply to multiple generators. Even Pop Goes the Weasel provides more instant regression from the perk itself if the generator you kick has at least 21% progress on it. There is just no reason to run Call of Brine at all if you’re after any meaningful regression in your build.
Some people might find themselves slightly persuaded by the extra information that Call of Brine provides, but that information is outclassed by Surveillance . Not only does Surveillance immediately tell you when a survivor starts repairing any affected generator, but it also synergizes with any perk that applies passive regression. Call of Brine does not . In order to get the auditory sound notifications from Call of Brine whenever a survivor performs a good skill check, you have to physically kick the generator yourself. As a result, you cannot apply Call of Brine by using Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Jolt , Oppression , or Hex: Ruin . Survivors can also prevent you from gaining any information from Call of Brine assuming they’re able to hit great skill checks, as well.
Not only is the information that you gain from Call of Brine sub-standard, but so is its regression . It is outclassed in all facets by every other perk in its category with regard to each benefit that it provides. I honestly cannot think of a single reason whatsoever to run this perk. The numbers just aren’t there, and there’s no reason to take it over Surveillance with how much more exceptional it is at providing information and synergizing with other regression perks.
Pop Goes the Weasel (PGTW)
Although Pop Goes the Weasel became a lot weaker after the nerf, it’s still very powerful when compared to other instant regression perks depending on how close a generator is to being completed. When you kick a generator while it is active, PGTW applies both the 20% penalty to current progress in addition to the base 2.5% penalty to maximum progress. Once a generator is 62.5% of the way to being completed, PGTW is equal in value to Pain Resonance when looking solely at instant generator regression. After that point, the regression from PGTW becomes stronger.
PGTW excels best at defending generators that are being prioritized by survivors. Prioritized generators are also most likely to have a lot of progress. If you are able to identify and create pressure around prioritized generators by downing survivors efficiently, you can typically ensure that with every hook you’re getting the most instant regression possible from a single perk slot. It only takes 56.25 seconds in order for a single survivor to reach 62.5% progress on one generator without factoring in any great skill checks, perks, or toolboxes. Two survivors can reach this threshold in 33 seconds. Due to Clown’s poor map mobility, you should be frequently coming across situations in your matches where, after ending a chase, there is at least one generator with this much progress. Assuming you were able to identify a prioritized generator, the only contingency that remains is if you physically have enough time to reach and kick it before it is completed.
Probably the biggest hurdle when it comes to getting value out of PGTW on Clown is the amount of time it takes to hook a downed survivor and then walk toward the prioritized generator. Depending on the number of survivors involved with finishing the generator while you were in chase, it is possible that you just do not have the time to reach the generator before it is completed. This is but one of the inherent flaws with generator-kicking perks on Clown. It is also the main reason why I would not recommend running PGTW by itself. Perks like PGTW, in my opinion, require the support of other perks in order to remain effective.
Through my own experience, I have found that Pain Resonance pairs together with PGTW very well. Pain Resonance has the advantage of telling you specifically which generator has the most progress. Further, being able to instantly apply a 15% regression penalty as you hook a survivor should usually give you the time you need in order to reach that priority generator before it is completed. It takes 13.5 seconds for a single survivor to undo the progress lost from Pain Resonance without factoring in any perks, toolboxes, or great skill checks. In that amount of time, you can walk 62.1 meters -worth of distance (4.6 m/s) before one survivor regains that lost progress.  Two survivors can regain that lost progress in 7 seconds, which is 32.2 meters-worth of distance. If there are two survivors involved, you might need to use a couple of yellow bottles in order to make sure you can push them off before they finish.
Lastly, it should be mentioned that the 45-second duration does require you to be cognizant of how long it is still active. Sometimes, you might feel tempted to try and commit to a chase near a priority generator before kicking it using PGTW. It is important to make sure that should you commit to a chase before kicking a generator with PGTW that you afford yourself enough of the remaining duration in order to return. If you end up over-extending without realizing it, you won’t actually have time to return to that priority generator before the duration expires. Even though it says it’s active for 45 seconds, it’s only technically available for 43.2 seconds when you also factor in how long it takes to kick the generator.
Rapid Brutality (RB)
For a lot of other killers, this perk bears a very significant downside. Clown, however, should seldom ever need to rely on Bloodlust on account of his bottles. You can’t both use your bottles as well as Bloodlust together. Every time you throw a bottle, the timer on Bloodlust mid-chase is reset to the very beginning. If Bloodlust is already active, it is immediately lost upon throwing a bottle. Clown’s yellow bottles are equal in strength to Bloodlust II. That, alone, is enough reason to disqualify Bloodlust as a viable chase alternative to Clown’s power. When you also factor in Clown’s pink bottles, the suggestion of ever relying on Bloodlust essentially becomes a detrimental strategy that could only be born out of desperation resulting from failure. In turn, there is virtually zero cost in disabling Bloodlust on Clown in order to reap the perk’s benefits.
As far as to how valuable Rapid Brutality is as a perk – individually, it’s honestly only decently-impactful. Your chase times will definitely be faster, as you will be hitting survivors in many instances before they are able to make it to certain key windows or pallets. It has synergy with your yellow bottles by compounding into a + 15% haste buff over a yellow bottle’s sole 10% when committing toward a follow-up hit. This being said, you would be able to replicate a similar outcome even faster by taking Coup de Grace or Save the Best for Last in its place without even including the possibility for Bloodlust at all. If you’re only looking to add one lethality perk to your build, I would somewhat advise against Rapid Brutality. However , if you’re looking to run multiple lethality perks in your build, things suddenly become different.
Rapid Brutality itself might not have the greatest influence on your overall chase times, but it has the very interesting effect of increasing the power of your other stronger lethality perks to entirely new thresholds. Both Coup de Grace and Save the Best for Last synergize very well with Rapid Brutality due to how it enables Clown to create even more opportunities of quickly downing survivors by keeping them vulnerable. Given a survivor being able to reach a key window or pallet can be the difference of anywhere from 20-30 seconds added to your chases, the ability to prevent that can be literally game-changing when it becomes possible to persistently do so at even more different areas across each map. When combined with either Coup de Grace, Save the Best for Last, OR BOTH , Rapid Brutality can even enable you to snowball wins dramatically faster than what would otherwise EVER be possible without it.
The manner in which you benefit from Rapid Brutality is almost entirely seamless. You don’t have to change up your strategy or adopt a different playstyle. As long as you are hitting survivors, then Rapid Brutality is giving you extra momentum for 10 seconds following each hit. With the only downside being the lack of gaining Bloodlust, you never have to worry about performing worse as a result of merely taking this perk. Stronger lethality perks like Coup de Grace or Save the Best for Last do not have this advantage. If you can’t preserve or maintain stacks of either Coup de Grace or Save the Best for Last, you’re likely going to perform worse with those perks than you would without them since you could be running a different perk in their stead.
Another interesting detail is that Rapid Brutality has a very transactional relationship with Play With Your Food . Normally, the problem with Play With Your Food on its own is that you’re usually trading in one of your few tokens just to get an injury on a healthy survivor. With Rapid Brutality, you get to essentially maintain that lost Play With Your Food stack for another 10 seconds as you continue to chase that same survivor.
Due to its comparatively weaker individual impact value in tandem with the synergy that it has with the other lethality perks, Rapid Brutality exists almost squarely as a supplementary lethality perk. With respect to my guide’s Build Template, this leaves it relegated to the Flex perk slot. It has fantastic synergy with Coup de Grace by itself – meaning that you can safely rely on using two of your other perk slots according to the Template on either regression or slowdown. However, I’d consider not running Rapid Brutality in the Flex perk slot if your Mandatory perk slot is used on Zanshin Tactics unless you want a lethality perk that doesn’t require you to change up how you play.
Sloppy Butcher (Sloppy)
This perk usually tends to be either hit or miss. It’s not that the perk is volatile, but it is dependent on factors outside of your control. When those factors aren’t present in your matches, it can actually be quite powerful. Sloppy Butcher excels best when the survivors in your matches don’t have any tools that either enable themselves to heal for free or without the assistance of others. Once assets like medkits, Inner Healing, Renewal, Pharmacy or Boon: Circle of Healing , etc. come into play, the value of Sloppy tapers off significantly.
What makes Sloppy so effective is how the mangled and hemorrhage debuffs interact with survivors that don’t have any of these aforementioned tools. Not only are they going to be dependent on another survivor to heal them, but the mangled debuff means that they are going to be doing it slower. This increased time spent healing has the chance to preoccupy survivors for longer so that they aren’t spending as much time repairing generators. Assuming you can track down where these survivors are healing, the hemorrhage debuff punishes these survivors by undoing all healing progress when you interrupt them. You can then convert this opportunity into a very quick down by chasing the injured survivor while they’re still vulnerable.
This is all assuming that the person playing Clown is able to consistently apply Sloppy Butcher to multiple survivors over an extended period of time. Beforehand, the Mangled and Hemorrhage debuffs used to have an indefinite duration. Following a nerf, both of these debuffs now last only 90 seconds after application. Without an above-average level of chase success, Clown will fall behind in macro pressure since survivors will stay on generators while injured and heal themselves after both debuffs have expired. Assuming they don’t heal and you do have above-average chase success, then you can snowball very easily off of their greed to repair generators.
Potential for very quick downs resulting from chasing injured survivors means that Sloppy has pretty strong synergy with hook-dependent gen regression perks such as Pain Resonance or Pop Goes the Weasel . Depending on how many survivors you have injured and how well you play in chase, you can snowball control of the match by only focusing on the injured survivors. Your chases should be short enough that the persistent pressure from your hook-dependent gen regression perks is capable of stalling out everyone else that is repairing generators. Once the pressure you put on the injured survivors starts resulting in people getting sacrificed, it becomes even harder for the remaining survivors to find a means to be full health again.
The downsides to Sloppy start to become apparent as more of those aforementioned tools are used. When survivors gain the ability to heal themselves, they are capable of undoing any pressure you put onto them without inconveniencing another survivor. This convenience directly translates into overall greater gen-efficiency. While you might still be slightly inconveniencing the one survivor that is healing themselves due to the mangled debuff, the other three are likely going to be dedicating their time toward generator progress. In these circumstances, Sloppy just isn’t going to be doing much at all to help you win the match. As Clown, you would be better off running either another gen regression/slowdown perk or a chase perk like Save the Best for Last .
A large part of what makes these aforementioned tools so troubling is that other than medkits it is impossible to tell if any survivor has one nor what it is that they have. You don’t know who or how many survivors may bring Circle of Healing, you don’t know where it gets placed or who placed it, you don’t know if someone is cleansing dull totems for Inner Healing, if they found a random medkit, or if they brought Pharmacy, there’s now Resurgence, etc. The most information you get as a killer to decide for yourself that bringing Sloppy is a good idea is the number of medkits you see in the lobby. However, even that information is extremely limited. You don’t know if those medkits are brown, yellow, green or purple. You don’t know if they brought Field Dressings with a Syringe or if instead of the Syringe they brought a Surgical Suture. They could have Syringe + Blighted Serum and use both in your very first chase (has happened to me a couple of times). Maybe they brought a Styptic. The smartest decision would be if in the lobby you see medkits you should expect the worst and likely run another perk. Even if you don’t initially see any medkits in the lobby, it’s important to remember that survivors can always swap to one at the last second before you can react to it, as well.
BBQ and Chilli (BBQ)
I don’t really feel like BBQ and Chilli is viable on Clown anymore with how the game has progressed. It is just way too weak and also inferior to a lot of the newer perks that have been released since it came out, such as Floods of Rage and Pain Resonance . Not only does the aura reading only last for 4 seconds, but the range restriction punishes you for hooking a survivor closer toward the center of a map. The 40 meter range limitation is just a radius – meaning that you are actually being restricted by a diameter of 80 meters when it comes to being able to see other survivor auras from where you hook a survivor. Assuming a survivor is far enough away for their aura to be seen, BBQ can still easily be countered by either Boon: Shadowstep , going into a locker, or running Distortion .
In the event that you can even see any survivor auras, that doesn’t necessarily mean you can use this information alone to make the smartest decision. If you see two survivors working on two different generators, how do you know which one to pressure? Do you pressure the one closest to you? Are you sure that the one farther away doesn’t have more progress? Without having recently checked both areas, you’re going to be reliant on some other form of information in order to know where it is best to go.
I just don’t see why one would take BBQ over Floods of Rage when Floods of Rage lasts 75% longer and doesn’t have the proximity range limitation. The only advantage BBQ has is that it doesn’t require a scourge hook and activates immediately after hooking a survivor. Even Pain Resonance would be better for information if you can identify which generator was affected since it also affords you extra time with the regression penalty. Although Pain Resonance doesn’t actually show you an aura, it always triggers where the survivors have put most of their attention in addition to slowing them down. This is a lot more than what can be said for BBQ when it comes to both opportunity cost and getting a similar effect from a single perk slot.
Some people might think that BBQ is worth running if you also bring Lethal Pursuer , but I don’t think that is productive. Committing two perk slots just to make one viable is a terrible idea when the alternative is running two strong perks that can be synergized together. Further, running Lethal Pursuer isn’t going to remove those same downsides that will prevent you from getting any value from BBQ whatsoever. At this point, you’re just putting even more eggs into a flimsy basket while also limiting what perk slots you have left for either gen defense or to help you in chase.
Lethal Pursuer (Lethal)
The very beginning of a match is probably one of the most crucial moments for Clown in the entire game. It is a point in time where you don't have any pressure and survivors still have their maximum resources (pallets and hook states). Once a match begins, your biggest priority is starting a chase and successfully downing a survivor as soon as possible. Lethal Pursuer can be useful for this, but locating survivors at the start without Lethal is surprisingly not that difficult. At the start of every match, both the killer and every survivor are scripted to spawn in certain patterns based on each other's proximity .
Depending on where the killer is set to spawn, there is a certain radius around the killer (not necessarily the killer’s terror radius) that prevents survivors from spawning too close. As the killer, you can use this to your advantage in order to eliminate needing to check certain areas once a match begins. Survivors usually spawn somewhat relatively close to you and are almost always next to a generator. When you get used to understanding survivor spawn patterns, it shouldn’t be difficult for you to identify areas of the map with survivors within the first 10 seconds of a match.
What makes Lethal Pursuer actually useful is not that it helps you start your first chase. Rather, it gives you the information necessary in order for you to ensure that your first chase is the most valuable . Although you can identify areas where survivors may spawn on your own, without Lethal, you still lack the knowledge of specifically which survivors are at what locations. As a result, you may end up pressuring an area of the map that has one survivor instead of an area of the map that has two . By the time you finish that first chase, those two survivors may have already finished a generator. It only takes 52.94 seconds for two survivors to do a generator without factoring in toolboxes, perks, or great skill checks. Having the direct foresight of where every survivor spawns can mean the difference in how many hooks you are able to get before the early generators are completed.
Looking at downsides, the most notable one is the aura reveal of Lethal expires 11 seconds after the match begins. After that point, the perk itself is close to being a dead perk slot. It is going to do nothing on its own in order to help you at any other point of the match. If you aren’t running any type of duration-based aura tool, then you are going to be playing almost the entire match with only three perks. When you run Lethal, you have to be absolutely sure that you pick a very strong first chase and down a survivor quickly . Having a poor early game where you aren’t getting many hooks is going to make coming back from behind in this situation even harder.
Clown also doesn’t have a lot of strong duration-based aura tools that would be affected by Lethal. The most powerful aura tool he has overall, by far, is his Cigar Box . To Clown’s detriment, the duration of his aura reading from Cigar Box is not independent from the duration of the invigoration buff itself. In turn, Cigar Box only has synergy with Clown’s add-ons that increase the invigoration duration and zero synergy with Lethal whatsoever. Tattoo’s Middle Finger is the only add-on that Clown has which would benefit from the increased aura duration, but even that appears to be lukewarm at best. You typically want your aura reading early at high wall tiles whereas Middle Finger only gives it to you as they run through the pink near the end . Since the aura reading is taking place at the end, neither the initial or extra duration is likely going to be relevant if you can establish line of sight while you are sped up by your yellow bottles.
Some might consider running an additional aura reading perk in order to get a benefit from Lethal’s secondary effect. The problem with this strategy is that there aren’t enough perk slots in order to make a build like this viable. You are only going to have two perk slots left to dedicate toward both gen defense and helping you in chase. The opportunity cost is just too great with regard toward making a build that has enough perks to protect your generators and end your chases quicker. If you are going to run Lethal in your build, your best bet is likely to run it without an additional aura reading perk by dedicating it to your flex perk slot.
Bamboozle (Bamboozle)
This perk looks a lot better on paper compared to how well it performs in actual matches. Being able to block a window for 16 seconds is a pretty significant time if you’re only chasing at a single tile. One might even think that it would be possible to execute a double-bottle combo in proximity to the blocked window for greater chase potential. Neither of these theories are consistent or practical. Most tiles that you come across with windows are either going to have two windows or will be connected in some way to another tile that has one. Survivors can either play around the unblocked window at that same tile or just abandon the tile entirely. Many strong windows are also situated at places where blocking the vault is irrelevant since the window itself is merely a stop-gap for a chase that leads in one direction. The Myers house on Haddonfield, Garden of Joy main building, Ormond main building, Ironworks of Misery main building 2nd floor, Father Campbell’s Church, and the Gideon Meat Plant tube are all some examples of where the only value you will gain from Bamboozle is the increased vault speed.
Further, Bamboozle interacts very negatively with Clown’s kit and how you set up your double bottles. Being required to vault the window in order to block it lowers Clown’s mobility by a considerable amount. It takes 1.44 seconds in order to vault a window with Bamboozle. Assuming you were previously sped up by your yellow bottle, the time it takes to vault the window is only going to leave you with 3.56 seconds remaining on your invigoration buff when you’re able to start moving again. This leaves you in a bad position if they choose to abandon the tile and need to rely on the invigoration in order to close distance. It can also punish you if they are still able to commit to a tile with two windows, reach your yellow cloud, and then use the invigoration to vault the second window before you can hit them. The latter can happen due to Clown’s invigoration expiring first coupled with the time required to vault the window. Whenever a survivor is invigorated and Clown is not, the difference in speed is only 0.2 m/s in Clown’s favor.
Even Killer Shack isn’t vulnerable to Bamboozle if the pallet is still available. All a survivor has to do is hug the side of the shack closest to the pallet and pre-drop the pallet after you vault the window. You might be able to get rid of the shack pallet quickly, but I wouldn’t consider this ability alone worth a whole perk slot. Although Killer Shack might be vulnerable to Bamboozle once the pallet is gone, smart survivors will not lead the chase here if it no longer has a pallet. Tools like Windows of Opportunity will also tell them early that the shack pallet is gone if they brought it.
The most practical use you can probably get out of Bamboozle is a supplementary increase to vault speed when paired alongside Superior Anatomy . Blocking the window would be completely irrelevant with this type of setup since the expectation is that you would be using your increased vault speed from both perks to catch survivors before they make it to either a window or pallet. However, this carries the downside of committing two perk slots toward helping you in only one area of chase potential. You could possibly make a build that ran Bamboozle, Superior Anatomy, and two gen regression/slowdown perks, but this would leave you without a perk slot for Zanshin Tactics . Without Zanshin, you’re going to be lacking a lot of constant information at tiles that is necessary on the majority of maps. It is doable, but not nearly as consistent or effective as a build that has Zanshin – especially when you consider that Bamboozle and Superior Anatomy ran together will only excel at certain specific tiles.
Superior Anatomy (SA)
Compared to Bamboozle , this perk is much more beneficial at the cost of a single perk slot. Bamboozle may increase your vaulting speed, but it pales in comparison to the speed increase you gain from Superior Anatomy . With just SA alone, it only takes 1.02 seconds to vault a window (whereas it takes 1.44 seconds with Bamboozle). This increased speed can be really useful whenever you’re chasing a survivor through a window that you cannot go around.
On certain maps, there are tiles with incredibly strong windows that require you to either brute force them by vaulting or avoid any chases where they are located. Choosing to do the latter can be very punishing for you if survivors constantly put a lot of pressure on these areas or if they lead chases in their direction. The Myers house on Haddonfield, Garden of Joy main building, Ormond main building, Ironworks of Misery main building 2nd floor, Father Campbell’s Church, and the Gideon Meat Plant tube are some examples of these tiles. SA enables you when chasing a survivor at these tiles to vault their key windows faster so you don’t lose as much distance. Depending on how much distance was initially between you and a survivor before they vaulted it, the increased vaulting speed you gain from SA could mean that you reach them before they make it to another window or a pallet. You can even throw a yellow bottle at the base of the window as you’re running up to vault it and you will be invigorated as the vault is completed.
Although SA is very strong in the situations where you would want it, what holds it back is that you may find these circumstances to be few and far in between. Vaulting windows at more conventional tiles may still not be fast enough. 1.02 seconds can be a lot of time spent vaulting a window when you consider that in the same timeframe you could have moved 5.17 meters using a yellow bottle. You might be able to do some setups where you throw a yellow bottle at a conventional window tile as you’re running up to vault the window, but I don’t see why you would do that when you could just execute a double-bottle combo around the window without vaulting it at all. A double-bottle combo is almost always going to be more consistent and nearly as fast (if not faster) when it comes to hitting survivors at a conventional tile. If you aren’t able to hit them before they run through the yellow cloud, then opting to throw a yellow bottle next to the window has the potential of helping the survivor that you’re chasing by making them faster.
Even when looking at those specific tiles I mentioned, there may be circumstances where with your increased vault speed you still can’t hit a survivor before they reach a second window. This can happen if there was too much initial distance between you and the survivor before vaulting the first window or if they have either Lithe or Balanced Landing . Due to its 30 second cooldown, SA is countered very heavily in a single chase by the presence of a second strong window that also must be vaulted. Waiting out the cooldown would take too long, so you might have to abandon chase if this happens.
You could probably combine Bamboozle with SA in order to make your vault speeds even faster, but that leaves you with one less perk slot in your build. With only two perk slots remaining, it might not be possible to design a perk loadout which will allow you to have the information from Zanshin Tactics in addition to having enough generator regression. You might have to sacrifice either Zanshin or some generator regression. SA and Bamboozle when combined will take your 1.02-second vault speed down to 0.765 seconds. This is a noticeable improvement, but I don’t know if I’d say this speed would ever be necessary outside of those specific strong tiles. Running just SA by itself is likely always going to enable you to make a stronger consistent build due to getting more value out of the perk slot you would be dedicating to Bamboozle.
Dissolution (Dissolution)
One might think that this perk would make Clown’s anti-looping ability even stronger than it is currently. Being able to instantly break a pallet after a survivor vaults it would theoretically allow your double-bottle combos to be lethal at probably every single pallet loop in the game . Without Dissolution , there are a few pallet loops where both your yellow and pink bottles used together aren’t enough to stop a survivor from reaching a pallet. Dissolution would allow you in these cases to remove the pallet after they vault it and then hit them shortly after. While this might seem like an incredibly strong asset to have for only a single perk slot, there are a few glaring downsides that make Dissolution unattractive if not unusable.
The first main issue Dissolution has is that the active timer is way too short . If survivors run in a straight direction to another tile as you’re recovering from successfully hitting them, it may take you anywhere from 10-15 seconds just to catch up to them. By the time this happens, you’re hardly going to have any time at all in order to both execute your double-bottle combo and follow through with trying to hit them around the dropped pallet. You may find situations where Dissolution expires seconds before a survivor vaults a pallet as a result. Assuming you need to reload between health states, getting value out of Dissolution is likely going to be impossible. You might be dependent on running either Sticky Soda Bottle or Cheap Gin Bottle in order to ensure that you have enough bottles for that reason.
Another problem (and probably the greatest) is that it actively tells survivors when Dissolution is active. This information is all survivors need for them to know that they should waste as much time as they can running forward and only pre-drop pallets to create distance. Smart survivors are not going to loop you when you have Dissolution. They are going to play around the fact that you are on a 20-second timer and are only able to get 0.6 meters closer for every second that passes. Dissolution is really held back by the lack of a surprise factor. Spirit Fury , by comparison, is a lot more effective in this regard since you do not know the killer has it until the perk first gains value. If Spirit Fury gave the same information Dissolution gives survivors whenever it was active, it would be borderline impossible to get any value from it.
The last concern is that Dissolution does nothing to help you at tiles that have either one or multiple windows. Even if the tile also has a pallet, survivors can counter Dissolution by pre-dropping it to create distance and then vaulting the window to separate you two even further. There is very little you can likely do in these situations because you are not in control of when survivors choose to drop the pallet. Going for the pallet stun isn’t necessary; all they need to do is use the pallet as a barrier. Once survivors are able to create enough initial distance, they can then just run forward and Dissolution will probably expire before you can catch back up to them at the next tile.
I can’t confidently recommend anyone run Dissolution on Clown due to how common these downsides are prevalent in practice. Being able to know as a survivor that Dissolution is active enables too much counterplay around its short available timer. Further, the majority of pallet loops are already unsafe without Dissolution to Clown’s double-bottle combos. You would be running Dissolution for only a small handful of loops that are spread across every map only to potentially not get value from this perk at any of them. It would be a lot more effective at doing its intended purpose if survivors either didn’t have the knowledge that it was active or if the timer was longer. Both of these details combined makes getting any type of value out of the perk too impractical.
Scourge Hook: Floods of Rage (Floods of Rage)
Compared to every other perk in the game that reveals survivor auras, Floods of Rage may possibly be the strongest. It doesn’t have a cooldown, lasts for a pretty significant amount of time (7 seconds), and is not limited by proximity like the similar perk BBQ and Chilli . The only requirement necessary in order to get value is that you hook a survivor on a scourge hook.  When it comes to exclusively focusing only 1-2 survivors, Floods of Rage excels at making sure you don’t lose track of your two priority targets. The only aura that is not revealed is the aura of the unhooked survivor. If you’re relatively close when the unhook takes place, you should be able to use the information Floods of Rage provides in order to start your next chase with a priority target and down them a lot faster. You can then use the information you gain from Floods of Rage when that survivor is unhooked in order to repeat the process and possibly snowball control of the match. All of this being said, there are a few details one should keep in mind if opting to run Floods of Rage in your build.
The most obvious one is that you have to actually hook a survivor on a scourge hook in order for the perk to have any effect. If you get a bad spread of scourge hook spawns, you may find yourself incapable of getting any value. Perk slot opportunity costs are very great – especially when those perk slots are dedicated toward information that isn’t being provided. As strong as Floods of Rage can be, it can also be borderline useless depending on how the map is generated. An information perk like BBQ in these situations would be more useful and consistent even if it would not be as powerful. That is not to say that I ever recommend running BBQ (reasons why in its own section), but it would definitely be more useful than Floods of Rage in your matches where the scourge hooks are spread very poorly.
One should also remember that you will not gain any value at all from Floods of Rage whenever hooking a survivor on a scourge hook for their third hook state. Floods of Rage does not show any survivor auras unless the hooked survivor is unhooked afterward. This reduces how much value it can provide at slowing down the game since you will be lacking that information to start your chases faster. Although you may be gaining the pressure that comes with killing a survivor, there may be circumstances where that pressure alone still isn’t enough to win the match. If you’re only at 1-2 generators remaining when you get your first kill, it’s still very possible that 2-3 survivors could escape through the exit gates.
Lastly, one should remember that this perk is shut down pretty heavily by Distortion . Unless you have any other tool to remove Distortion stacks (such as Cigar Box ), Floods of Rage is likely going to do very little to help you find survivors that are running it. They will have guaranteed protection for 3 hooks and may likely earn more stacks to protect them for longer. I wouldn’t go as far as to say that you shouldn’t run this perk at all without a tool to remove Distortion stacks, but you will definitely get punished if you come across survivors with Distortion and you don’t.
Floods of Rage is capable of fitting into most consistent builds as a flex perk. It doesn’t carry any dependency on other perks in order to be worth running. Lethal Pursuer may increase the duration by 2 seconds, but it lasts long enough on its own. If you’re looking for strong perk synergies, Pain Resonance pairs extremely well with Floods of Rage. You gain immediate generator regression out of your scourge hooks in addition to seeing survivor auras after the hooked survivor is freed. Pain Resonance also allows you to actually gain some value out of placing a survivor on a scourge hook for their third hook state. While it may be risky to run two scourge hook perks together due to the possibility of getting a bad hook spread, the independent strengths of these two perks alone are strong enough to at least consider taking that risk.
I’m All Ears (IAE)
I’m All Ears initially seems like on the surface it might be really useful in chase. Being able to see a survivor’s aura for 6 seconds after they perform a rushed action (typically, a vault) might make one assume that this perk could serve as a replacement to Cigar Box so that one does not have to run the add-on. Cigar Box does a lot more than help you plan your chases properly around high wall tiles. More on Cigar Box is explained in my add-on tier list video below. Further, the way IAE is designed can make the information you gain from it worthless.
IAE’s biggest problem is that it is too easily triggered along with having too long of a cooldown. Any rushed action within 48 meters will activate IAE and then put it on cooldown 6 seconds later for 40 seconds . With how long your chases should be lasting, this is effectively the same thing as putting it on cooldown for the remainder of your entire chase. You cannot afford to chase the same survivor until it is off cooldown. If a single chase is lasting long enough for you to get multiple uses out of IAE, then your chases aren’t ending fast enough and it is clearly not doing anything to help you accomplish that.
It is surprisingly very easy for IAE to get triggered in a way where it provides zero value. 48 meters is 50% larger than your terror radius. You could be walking across the map to check a generator, get seen by a survivor outside your terror radius, and then IAE gets activated after they fast vault the window to gain distance. It will then be on cooldown for 40 seconds – which may be the majority of your next chase. This also means that it won’t be available at the start of that chase in the event the beginning is when you need the information most.
There is also the possibility that you go against survivors who have Distortion . IAE’s 48 meter range coupled with its 40 second cooldown means that it is possible for survivors running Distortion to safely trigger IAE from a distance where you can’t immediately respond to them. They can then restore their Distortion stacks later on at some point in the match. It takes 120 seconds for IAE by itself to deplete every Distortion stack from one survivor. Without another aura-revealing tool to deplete Distortion stacks, IAE is likely to never do anything at all.
I would personally not recommend anyone run IAE at all on Clown with the intention of designing a consistent perk build. In the event that the cooldown of IAE is reduced or a new perk comes out that allows you to reduce the cooldown of other perks, this has the potential to change. With how easy it is for survivors to trigger it in a way where the aura-reading doesn’t help you, you would be better off getting this type of information from Cigar Box, instead. You could then dedicate that perk slot to either a flex perk or another generator regression perk for greater pressure.
Hex: Ruin (Ruin)
Ruin is in an absolutely atrocious state for Clown after the nerf. It might arguably be one of the worst hex perks for Clown in the entire game. Automatic generator regression might initially seem really attractive to some people, but the base regression speed is way too slow . It takes 4 seconds of real time for Ruin to regress 1 second of generator progress. A generator at 50% progress will take 3 minutes and 20 seconds in order to regress to 0%. Survivors can capitalize on your poor map mobility by either starting to cleanse the totem when it is outside your terror radius or they can ignore it entirely and rush the generators. You are not simultaneously-capable of chasing one survivor, stopping another survivor from cleansing Ruin, or the third and fourth survivors from working on generators so that they don't regress.
With how weak Ruin is as an actual perk, there is very little reason to run Hex: Undying in order to ensure that survivors don’t remove it too early. You would be committing an extra perk slot to protect a perk that is below sub-par. Survivors may not even care to remove it due to it being near powerless. You would be better off instead running either two different generator regression perks besides Ruin + Undying or running a stronger hex perk along with Undying. The fact that Ruin removes itself after one survivor dies means that survivors are incentivized even more to ignore Ruin entirely the longer a match goes on. Even when it is active, there are likely going to be many matches where it feels like a dead perk slot due to how little it is helping you along with how little survivors are concerned about it.
Another major problem is that Ruin has negative synergy with all generator-kicking perks. Until Ruin is either removed or cleansed, you are incapable of kicking any generator that a survivor is not working on. This means you can’t apply perks like Eruption , Overcharge , Pop Goes the Weasel , Call of Brine , or Nowhere to Hide as long as Ruin is still active. Further, generator-blocking perks like Deadlock , Dead Man’s Switch , Thrilling Tremors , or Merciless Storm also prevent Ruin from passively regressing a generator, as well. Opting to run Ruin essentially restricts your build from having access to most of the game’s stronger generator slowdown perks in exchange for a volatile perk with really poor regression. The only viable generator regression perks you have access to in a build with Ruin are Pain Resonance and Jolt .
Hex: Devour Hope (Devour Hope)
Compared to any other hex perk that is available, Hex: Devour Hope is probably the most powerful hex perk there is for Clown. It doesn’t really do much at all for you at the start of a match, but over time it ramps up harder than any other perk in the game. You can end a match very easily by running Devour Hope as long as you get at least 5 hooks before it is cleansed. Even if Devour Hope is cleansed before you are able to mori a survivor, the level of chase pressure you gain at 3 stacks before hooking two survivors afterward should be great enough to snowball control of the match. Unless you spread your hook states really poorly, you should be able to prioritize one or two survivors that are on death hook at this point and secure some very quick kills by sacrificing them.
Getting value out of Devour Hope is mostly dependent on two factors. 1. Survivors do not find your totem early, and 2. you need to hook survivors quickly. Actively defending your generators isn’t as much of a priority as getting in chases, hooking survivors, and gaining stacks. Clown’s poor map mobility means that you likely aren’t going to have the convenience of defending your totem whenever you commit to a chase. You have to hope that the hex doesn’t get cleansed/booned while you’re busy chasing a survivor. Further, extending the duration of your chases by making mistakes is only going to provide more opportunities for survivors to either finish generators or come across the hex.
It should be mentioned that survivors have become a lot more passively aware of hex perks ever since boon perks were released. Boon perks have created an attractive incentive for many survivors to actively seek out totem spawns in order to get perk value. Probably the most popular boon that survivors like to run is Boon: Circle of Healing . Survivors will typically look for a totem either at the start of a match or when they are injured. Assuming that they look for a totem at the start of a match, there is a chance they accidentally come across it before you even get your first hook. Losing a hex perk extremely early is probably one of the most glaring reasons as to why even a hex perk like Devour Hope will never be consistently viable. No matter its potential, the perk slot is still invalidated once the hex is removed.
One could try to supplement this weakness by also running Hex: Undying and it might increase how long Devour Hope is active by a decent margin. Losing a hex totem near the very start of the match won’t be as punishing as long as the second hex totem isn’t found shortly after. The main problem with this strategy is that you still can’t effectively defend either hex totem and you are going to be down two perk slots instead of one if both totems are removed. You might have more success at getting value from Devour Hope with this perk combo, but it requires you to risk playing with only two functioning perks if survivors counter it.
If you’re not concerned with your build having a more consistent flex perk, Devour Hope could fit into the flex slot of your build rather well. As a flex perk, it is not the entire focus of how you can expect to win a match. Most of your strength is still going to be centered on both of your generator regression perks as well as likely Zanshin Tactics if you include it. If survivors cleanse Devour Hope in this type of build, you’re still playing with three perks but the downside won’t be as noticeable because of how strong those three perks should be.
Overcharge (Overcharge)
This perk is unique from other regression perks in a lot of ways. It is not hook-dependent, can be applied to multiple generators, and it affects both a generator’s passive regression speed in addition to offering the chance of an instant regression penalty. All of these aspects can make Overcharge a strong supplementary generator regression perk in your build. It synergizes very well with generator-kicking perks like Pop Goes the Weasel . You can also run it in a build with Pain Resonance since most survivors will tap the Pain Ress’d generator in order to stop the passive regression. Although this perk has the potential to be quite powerful when paired with many different slowdown perks, it has a few downsides that exist beyond your control.
Looking first at the instant regression penalty, it will only apply if the survivor who touches the generator doesn’t land the skill check. In this scenario, it is capable of instantly regressing a generator with as much power as Pain Resonance (10% base + 5% from Overcharge). The problem is that survivors who are used to landing great skill checks are not likely to experience any increased difficulty in landing the Overcharge skill check. At best, you might surprise one of these survivors a single time because they didn’t expect it and afterward they will know to pay attention. There isn’t really a practical way for Clown to make landing the Overcharge skill check harder beyond running Unnerving Presence and keeping the generator within his terror radius (an impractical solution). When you run Overcharge on Clown, you’re basically reliant on the survivors you play against to struggle with great skill checks.
Even the passive regression has a pretty noticeable flaw. While it may eventually ramp up to twice the base regression speed after 30 seconds, the initial regression speed is slower than the base regression speed for the first 12 seconds. Survivors that start working on an Overcharged generator within a short period of time and can land the skill check will be punishing you for running Overcharge due to the decreased initial regression. In turn, you have to make sure that there aren’t any survivors hiding in proximity. You could probably locate any nearby survivors if you’re also running Nowhere to Hide or Cigar Box in order to prevent this from happening.
What makes Overcharge only a supplementary regression perk instead of a primary regression perk is that Clown lacks too much agency in order to get value from either the instant or passive regression. The only agency you have as Clown is kicking the generator and chasing any nearby survivors you find. Survivors can both stop the passive regression before it ramps too high and ward off the instant regression by landing the skill check. This means that survivors are effectively the ones in control of how much value you gain from using your own regression perk. Compared to any other generator regression perk, this gravely impacts Overcharge’s consistency on account of the fact that other regression perks do not have this problem.
One new and unique benefit that Overcharge now has compared to other instant regression perks is that the failed skillcheck does not count as an ‘instant regression event.’ This means that one can run Overcharge alongside another generator-kicking perk like Pop Goes the Weasel and gain the instant regression value of Overcharge without worrying about it potentially triggering a generator’s instant regression event cap.
The level of agency survivors possess at controlling the regression you gain from Overcharge means that it might work best in your flex perk slot . Running stronger instant regression perks that can be supplemented by Overcharge instead of relying on Overcharge as one of your two primary regression perks should allow you to do better in situations where gen-efficient survivors aren’t fazed by the skill checks. I wouldn’t recommend running Overcharge if you only plan on running two generator regression perks and intend on dedicating your flex perk slot to something else.
Nowhere to Hide (NtH)
Nowhere to Hide is mostly a convenience perk on Clown more than anything. It is a fantastic flex perk since it synergizes with any gen-kicking regression perk. Survivors that attempt to hide near a generator will be visible for you to chase and might even give you a free health state before they start creating distance. The lack of a cooldown also enables you to kick multiple generators in succession and gain information each time. Snowballing off of this information becomes very possible if you play well in chase and down survivors quickly. There should be very little downtime in between you hooking survivors, kicking generators, and starting your next chase.
As strong as all of this may be, NtH remains a convenience perk on Clown due to the versatility of his Cigar Box add-on. You can already achieve a very similar result as NtH with Cigar Box by throwing a yellow bottle at a generator as you run up to kick it. This also has the benefit of making you faster as you move toward any possible nearby survivors. NtH’s range may be 8 meters larger when compared to Cigar Box’s 16 meters, but NtH requires you to give up a whole perk slot just to get that 8 extra meters. Additionally, Cigar Box fulfills a much greater role by helping you in chase at high wall tiles. NtH does not have this advantage and could otherwise be replaced by a perk that would synergize better with the rest of your build.
The 24-meter range of NtH can still be particularly useful if you happen to play on any indoor or vertical map (Lery’s Memorial, Gideon Meat Plant or Midwich). The aura reading you gain after kicking a generator persists through floors and can potentially reveal survivors that are below you. It could also help you out more at certain structures like the Disturbed Ward main building and Ormond main building. These structures are large and possess a lot of side rooms that would make reading auras after kicking the nearby generator very effective. Survivors possibly won’t expect that you know where they are and you can probably begin your chases with a quick health state because of it.
It is still important to keep in mind that NtH is just as vulnerable as every other aura-reading tool to any asset survivors may have to block their auras from being seen. You may end up conditioning yourself to only look for auras after kicking generators and wind up in situations where you still can’t find any survivors. Anything from Distortion , Boon: Shadowstep , Off the Record , or even simply hiding in lockers is enough to prevent NtH from generating any value. Hiding in lockers is probably the most prevalent as you cannot afford to randomly open lockers searching for a survivor that may not even be at that tile. Even Whispers wouldn’t be able to counter survivors hiding in lockers since you wouldn’t have the certainty that Whispers isn’t instead being triggered by a nearby survivor outside of a locker and running Distortion.
NtH is a great flex perk if you aren’t running Cigar Box, have a generator-kicking build, and need help tracking survivors. I would even run it if my only generator-kicking perk was Pop Goes the Weasel . You don’t need to bring perks that incentivize you to kick multiple generators like Eruption or Overcharge for NtH to be valuable. What makes NtH worth running is being able to use the information you gain from it to start your next chase. As long as you can use NtH to start your next chase, it becomes efficient due to how much it reduces your downtime.
No Way Out (NWO)
Endgame perks are particularly risky on Clown when compared to slowdown perks due to their reliance on you performing well before all generators are completed. By virtue of even running an endgame perk, you are sacrificing a perk slot that could make the rest of the match beforehand significantly easier. No Way Out is no exception to this. The greatest drawback present with NWO is the requirement of getting 1 hook on each survivor in order to get the most value from the perk. Spreading your hooks to such a degree puts the least form of pressure onto gen-efficient survivors. It drastically reduces your ability to either sacrifice and/or put one or more survivors on death hook before all of the generators are completed. Compared to the rest of the match, the post-gen phase is always the shortest and the one where survivors have the potential to be the most in control. Once the exit gates are 99’d, there is essentially nothing you can do to stop survivors from leaving on a whim. Your ability to pressure even one survivor becomes threatened as the other remaining survivors are no longer pre-occupied and can move in to protect them.
NWO is likely only going to get its most value in matches that you were already going to win without it. If you’re capable of both rotating hooks to get 4 stacks of NWO in addition to either killing and/or putting one or more survivors on death hook before every generator is completed, chances are you would have won using a different perk, as well. What really holds NWO back is that all it really does is slow down the quickest period of the game. Further, it doesn’t even increase your lethality during that period of time. Healthy survivors are still going to require two health states, injured survivors can heal, and other survivors can still bodyblock. Unless you come across an injured survivor, you could possibly spend most of NWO chasing one survivor in order to get a single down. It is for these reasons that it’s hard for me to see NWO as an endgame perk that can stand on its own. Putting it in your flex perk slot is thus not practical. Hex: No One Escapes Death would likely perform better as an endgame perk in the flex slot since you could use the increased lethality to target a survivor already on death hook.
You could possibly supplement NWO into an endgame-centered build along with NOED, Terminus , and a flex perk slot (like Zanshin). When running such a build, it is important to keep in mind that endgame perk setups lack any ability to stall out the most crucial stage of the match – the generator-phase. Unless you plan on slugging out all four survivors during the post-gen phase, you need to kill at least one survivor before all generators are completed in order to ensure that you have enough time to chase and down the other three. This means you can’t be making mistakes in chases and you should first prioritize hooking only 1-2 survivors. It is unnecessary to immediately rush 4 stacks of NWO if that increases the chance that the post-gen phase begins without any survivors dead or even on death hook.
Without any inherent ability to stall out a match during the generator-phase, endgame-centered builds are not capable of being consistent across the majority of your matches. Gen-efficient teams that include survivors who are strong in chase are going to finish the generators way too quickly. By the time the post-gen phase starts in this type of match, you may be lucky to have 3-4 hooks, 2-3 stacks of NWO, no kills, and nobody on death hook. You also might have a chance at forcing two kills in a match like this if you slug, but this can still be countered. Once survivors see that you have NOED and Terminus, they are going to try and prevent you from slugging by spreading out and hiding at different areas of the map. They are also likely at this point to assume you’re running an endgame build and will check for NWO. Depending on the size of the map, both exit gate locations, and how early NWO was triggered, some survivors might still have a chance at escaping with proper coordination. They can try to pick up the slugged survivors or work on either exit gate once NWO expires.
Hex: No One Escapes Death (NOED)
A lot of people typically end up choosing Hex: No One Escapes Death on Clown for the wrong reason. It is not an almighty win condition that will automatically win your matches for you. If you do not get a lot of hooks before all generators are completed, there’s a good chance with this perk that you don’t walk away with more than one kill. Survivors will either abandon whoever it is that you hook during the endgame or they will cleanse the NOED and coordinate a bodyblocked hook save. The latter is especially more likely to happen after the recent nerf since it now shows an aura.
In order to get the most value out of NOED, you either need one survivor already dead or you need to be in chase with a healthy survivor who is on death hook right when the last generator is completed. You want to down that survivor very quickly because you do not have a lot of time afterward to defend the exit gates. The difference during the post-gen phase between hooking a survivor who has already been hooked once instead of twice is that you don’t have to camp a survivor who has been hooked twice. If you can find another survivor on death hook before they manage to open an exit gate or cleanse NOED, then yes, NOED alone is capable of giving you two kills very easily.
Having to camp a survivor because they are not on death hook during the post-gen phase means that you can’t defend your exit gates, can’t chase another survivor, nor can you defend NOED from being cleansed. It puts you in a very restricted position where all NOED is really doing for you is ensuring that survivors either find NOED before they hook save or they just leave through the gates. Further, in the event that they find the NOED spawn shortly after the last generator is completed, you now don’t have that tool to help you create any endgame pressure when you were already behind. The opportunity cost of running NOED is felt even more in these situations since you could have run a different perk to stall out the generator phase, instead. If you are going to run NOED in your build, it needs to supplement your other perks and it can not be a perk that you are always relying on to win for you by itself.
One nice benefit of running NOED is that it is probably the most effective perk for countering the survivor perk Adrenaline . Some people might suggest Terminus , but what makes Terminus inferior to NOED at countering Adrenaline is that Terminus doesn’t do anything at all for you if a survivor is healthy when it activates. They will still get the speed boost and you will still have to hit them two times in order to down them. The only time where the lethality between Terminus and NOED is similar is when a survivor is still injured right as the last generator is completed. Even then, NOED still has slightly greater lethality due to the 4% haste buff.
My best suggestion for slotting NOED in a consistent build would be according to the template. You would run Zanshin Tactics , NOED in the flex perk slot, and two generator slowdown perks. The slowdown perks also need to have good synergy and have to provide you with enough stall to either kill one survivor or put two survivors on death hook before the last generator is completed. Perks like NWO or Terminus are unable to fit into a consistent build outside of Midwich Elementary School since a significant number of maps are either too large for you to get value from them or they don’t stall out the match as long as generator slowdown. You also can’t reliably replace Zanshin for other maps due to how map tiles are randomly generated. Assuming you bring a map offering, you could replace Zanshin on Midwich Elementary School with one of these two perks due to the map’s small size, the predictability of its tiles, and the proximity of the exit gates.
Terminus (Terminus)
Terminus has very limited uses on Clown by itself. It is only active at the end of the match during the post-gen phase. With how short the post-gen phase can be, you aren’t going to have a lot of time to apply pressure. Further, your lethality is still not increased whatsoever against healthy survivors. It may leave them broken after injury, but you’re still going to need to hit them twice in order to down them. Depending on how spread out the exit gates are when the last generator is completed, all you may be doing by running Terminus is encouraging survivors to open the gates and leave even sooner.
Healthy survivors can also open the gate before attempting hook saves in order to avoid the broken status effect. The endgame collapse lasts for up to 4 minutes if an alive survivor is incapacitated (downed/hooked), which is plenty of time for survivors to recover in between saves if they have to trade hooks. Unless multiple survivors happen to be injured right as the last generator is completed, you might end up playing matches where this perk doesn’t help you down survivors faster whatsoever.
While some people might see Terminus as a counter to the survivor perk Adrenaline , all it actually does is prevent injured survivors with the perk from healing. They are still going to get the speed boost if they were injured, and if they were healthy you still need to hit them twice . Preventing an injured survivor that you are actively chasing from healing right as the last generator is completed may be significant for winning that one chase, but it is too circumstantial and inconsistent when compared to the benefits of other perks. NOED , for example, would allow you to effectively end chases at the exact same speed while also giving you even greater lethality against the other survivors.
I wouldn’t recommend ever running Terminus in a consistent build even as a flex perk. By itself, Terminus is just too weak and running a perk that synergizes with it would break the template. If you’re looking for an endgame flex perk that can stand on its own, NOED would be a much better choice. It does a much better job at increasing your endgame lethality since all survivors can be downed in a single hit unless they have the endurance status effect. Comparatively, the only aspect of Terminus that is stronger than NOED is that Terminus can’t be removed until after the endgame collapse has already begun.
Spirit Fury (Spirit Fury)
I think Spirit Fury by itself maybe has some potential on Clown when it comes to countering certain strong pallets. Due to how much time it takes, you don’t typically want to kick pallets while you are in a chase since you could be using that time to get closer to the survivor. However, there are some pallets where the choice to kick it is beyond your control. You may be playing on a map where there is a pallet that blocks a hallway or creates an unstoppable loop. In these situations, I think it may be possible to utilize Spirit Fury’s efficiency if you can get these survivors to pallet stun you while it is active. This can shorten your chase times at these areas significantly since survivors won’t gain any extra distance by forcing you to kick the pallet after waiting out the stun.
Meeting Spirit Fury’s requirement of broken pallets would likely best be done in between downing survivors near already-dropped weaker pallets. This way, you’re not stalling out your chases. There is still the concern of generators being completed before you can apply instant regression/slowdown, but this is probably the best opportunity you are going to have to do it. You can’t afford to make your chases last longer just so you can have Spirit Fury ready when you don’t even know when you’re going to use it. Choosing to do so would be completely contradictory to the purpose of bringing Spirit Fury to begin with. This is especially true when considering as Clown you aren’t in control of when Spirit Fury is actually triggered.
Speaking of this lack of control, it becomes a lot harder for Spirit Fury to synergize with other perks for that very reason. A lot of people like to pair Enduring with Spirit Fury since the reduced stun duration makes it substantially harder for survivors to create distance after Spirit Fury activates. This combo works well when survivors play into it, but it hurts you even more when they play around it. Choosing to run Spirit Fury and Enduring together means survivors can now counter two of your perks with the exact same strategy. You are going to bear the burden of opportunity cost a lot harder since you could be running either different chase perks, more generator slowdown/regression, or even info perks to start your next chases faster. Just the sheer opportunity cost of running Spirit Fury and Enduring together is enough to break my optimal build template. You can’t afford to run less than two slowdown/regression perks for stalling out the game and you can’t consistently play across most maps without Zanshin Tactics due to random tile generation.
As interesting as Spirit Fury may be, I’m still hesitant to recommend it even as a flex perk due to the requirement of kicking pallets and the agency survivors have over triggering it. The moment survivors realize you have Spirit Fury, it is possible for them to play in a way where it never activates again. It’ll even be useless against those certain strong pallets since there is nothing preventing survivors from pre-dropping them. It might be fun to experiment or play around with, but as it stands it’s too impractical. Regardless, I still think there might be some potential hidden beneath the layers.
Enduring (Enduring)
There is seldom a reason to ever run Enduring on Clown since there isn’t any benefit gained from being pallet stunned to begin with. If you are never pallet stunned in a match, this perk does literally nothing. Even being stunned once or twice is not enough to suddenly validate using this perk. If you are getting stunned by pallets, that is a mistake which is completely within your control. Unless you are swinging through a pallet trying to hit a survivor with Dead Hard before they use it, there is just no acceptable reason for you to get stunned by a pallet. In that one situation you are essentially punished no matter what you do, but other than that there is no other reason for you to ever be stunned by a pallet – especially with Zanshin Tactics .
Clown’s ability to hit survivors around the majority of pre-dropped pallets means that you shouldn’t be desperate to swing into a pallet if a survivor is camping it. How you play around their choice to camp a pallet is by placing your yellow bottle early, baiting the survivor into accidentally pre-dropping the pallet by pretending to walk under it, turning to throw your pink bottle on the other side, and then chasing them around the loop. More on this is covered under the early fundamental chapters of the guide.
Some people like to run Enduring with Spirit Fury , but I heavily advise against this. Not only are you basically adopting a build that caters to a subpar playstyle, but you enable survivors to simultaneously counter two of your perks with the very same strategy. Once smart survivors see that you have both Enduring and Spirit Fury, they are going to pre-drop every pallet for the rest of the match. They might have already planned to do this to begin with, as people consider pre-dropping pallets an effective way to last longer in chase against Clown. If that happens to be the case, in those situations you’d be literally throwing away two perk slots against survivors who didn’t even know you brought those perks.
Do not run this perk. I don’t think it is good even as a flex perk on its own. The synergy with Spirit Fury can be easily invalidated the exact same way as if you didn’t have it for more free and wasted opportunity cost. You shouldn’t be getting hit with pallet stuns to begin with – just play better. It is basically the chase-equivalent to Lightborn with more prevalent counterplay available to survivors. In the same way that survivors stop going for flashlight saves when they see the killer has Lightborn, survivors stop going for pallet stuns when they see either Enduring and/or Spirit Fury. There are so many better flex perks you could choose from. If I was looking for a flex perk that would make my chases faster, I’d much sooner run Save the Best for Last .
Starstruck (Starstruck)
I’ve done some fun and gimmicky strategies with Starstruck , but ultimately it’s not very strong or consistent on Clown. Starstruck’s duration is just too short in order to reliably end a chase after hooking a survivor but before it expires. Survivors will see that you have Starstruck the moment you pick up a survivor and can use that time to create too much distance. If you are on an extremely small map like Dead Dog Saloon, you might be able to make something happen. Other than that, you are going to have problems both finding survivors and actually downing them within 30 seconds due to your poor map mobility. If you are able to hit a survivor within 30 seconds of hooking another survivor, then chances are the second survivor played poorly unless you downed that first survivor right next to a hook. Avoiding Starstruck is a lot harder when you can hook survivors immediately since the other survivors aren’t able to use that time to run outside your terror radius.
Starstruck also has the same problem as other perks (like Spirit Fury ) where once survivors know you have it, they can play around it for the rest of the match. After the first down, you can be absolutely sure that there are going to be zero survivors within your terror radius for every other down going forward. There’s just no way outside of sending yourself to really small maps in order to ensure that you can hit survivors while they are still exposed.
I wouldn’t consider running this perk at all in a consistent or optimal build – even as a flex perk. It does not stand well on its own, has too many dependencies, and is too easily countered. The majority of the maps also make it too hard to get any value from it. If I was looking for a more consistent flex perk that makes survivors exposed, I would go with Make Your Choice . MYC lasts 2x the duration and identifies for you which survivor is exposed based on who screams. Other than that, I would pick a different flex perk altogether.
How to Make a Strong Perk Build
Optimal Clown Perk Template
After many months of trying different perk combinations, I have currently settled on the above template as the go-to formula for designing different Clown perk builds. There is a fair degree of customization and variety available even when considering each perk slot’s criteria. I want to be clear that this template will not help you design a perfect build that will never lose. Every build has flaws somewhere within the kit as a result of sacrificed opportunity cost. What this template will do is guide you toward making your own really powerful and consistent Clown builds for the purpose of killing survivors and winning as many matches as you can. Below, I will explain  each perk category and provide you with a list of some relevant perks that you could choose. There may be perks that I do not list which happen to fall under said category due to the overall sheer number of perks. These perk examples are more to provide you with concrete choices that you can build off on your own.
Mandatory
This particular perk slot is reserved for one of two perks which will have the greatest overall impact on your Clown performance alone. For different reasons, both Zanshin Tactics and Coup de Grace are invaluable perks on Clown in almost literally every single build . It doesn’t matter what your Flex Perk or Slowdown #1/#2 Perks are; Zanshin Tactics and Coup de Grace will interact perfectly with the rest of your build without any dependency on your other perk slots. That being said, each of these two perks serves a different purpose.
Zanshin Tactics will drastically increase the potential for your overall momentum by outlining key areas of each tile in front of you. Knowing where windows and pallets are is very useful for placing your bottles properly and at the right time. Without this information or knowledge, possibilities for shorter chases cannot become realized and your chases, as a result, become longer by default. Many people don’t really recognize or appreciate Zanshin Tactics at first glance, but its strength becomes more appreciated once you’ve adapted to a point where you’re not kicking as many pallets every match. However, it is also important to remember that despite this benefit, Zanshin Tactics is still just an information perk. While it might help one conceptualize how to play out a chase so that one can down survivors faster, it doesn’t actually increase the extent of Clown’s lethality beyond what would be technically possible without it.
Conversely, Coup de Grace is to be run explicitly for its impact on increasing one’s lethality. The impact that the extra 80% lunge range has on your chases is unrivaled by any other perk. There isn’t a single other perk that enables Clown to get hits as easily as Coup de Grace. Having that extra lunge range alongside both your yellow and pink bottles makes avoiding some hits at many tiles borderline impossible. Many people dislike that you only gain the benefit every time you lose a generator. This is actually a really good thing. Perks which activate off of losing generators are more flexible on Clown since losing generators is always going to happen due to his poor map mobility. If Coup de Grace required hooks, that would make it extremely hard to get value from it when one is behind and needs it most.
More information on each perk is independently available within the Perk Discussion section of this guide. As far as to which of these perks you should run, the answer to that question is strictly up to you. Again, each perk serves a different purpose. We’re essentially comparing apples and oranges, here, because both are taking up the same spot in the basket. Yet, either one of these, in my opinion, is absolutely necessary for composing an incredibly powerful build. That’s not to suggest that you can’t have strong builds without these perks – it’s just that the impact of Coup de Grace and Zanshin Tactics on a build are second to none but each other.
Flex Perk
This perk slot has the freedom of being dedicated to almost quite literally anything. You shouldn’t necessarily need more than two complimentary generator slowdown perks, but there is no harm in running a third if it synergizes with the other two. You could also run a perk that gives you aura reading or information. Perhaps you feel like your build needs a form of utility to deal with survivor healing speeds or the items they carry.
There are a vast number of viable perk possibilities you could choose. What is important to keep in mind is that you do not pick a perk that is not independent and cannot stand on its own. A flex perk is meant to bend around your other perk choices and not the other way around. Further, the rest of your build is meant to be capable of standing on its own without any reliance on what the flex perk provides.

Slowdown Perks #1 & #2
The bread and butter to every Clown build is what two generator slowdown/regression perks you choose to run. You can’t really afford to run only one slowdown/regression perk due to the base gen-efficiency that survivors are capable of achieving (especially on medium to larger-sized maps). Running two slowdown/regression perks lets you consistently stall more matches for longer so that you have a better chance of getting more hooks and sacrifices before all of the generators are done. It also enables you to consider combining two slowdown/regression perks with similar effects for a greater outcome.
In my experience, I’ve found hook-dependent instant regression perks are probably the strongest of the regression perks available for Clown. These perks require you to have fast chases and snowball in order to do well. In return, you are rewarded with an exceptional level of pressure on your generators. Perks that block generators are also valuable and will give you some extra variety in your build design. They excel at providing you with support at different stages of the match depending on when you need the help. When running generator-blocking perks, just keep in mind that a blocked generator cannot passively regress or suffer from any instant regression penalties.

Guide Writer's BEST Choice (Current Favorite NON - Template Meta Build)
Last Updated: March 21, 2025
Perks: Dying Light, Scourge Hook: Gift of Pain, Surge/Jolt, Play With Your Food (flex)
Add-ons: Cigar Box/Flask of Bleach , Garish Makeup Kit
Although I initially only wanted to primarily recommend builds to my audience within the Guide which conform to the Guide Template , this one specific build is so much of an exception that I am putting it above the recommended Template build for the purpose of more strongly encouraging its usage due to its effectiveness. If your main concern is winning as many matches as you can, this is the first and number one go-to build I would recommend for anyone before anything else.
The main problem that Clown and a lot of other weaker killers have is that they cannot compete in macro efficiency regarding their objective goal compared to that of the survivors. This is because survivors who are efficient and coordinated at repairing generators will usually outpace Clown at the speed in which he can acquire enough necessary hooks to secure 3 kills before they are able to escape. Clown does not have map mobility and this means he can’t push survivors off of generators. As a result, this is how Clown is left with this negative disproportionate imbalance of generator repair speeds vs. hook times even though he is a killer with a very strong chase power.
With this build, Clown is able to circumvent the fact that he lacks the mobility to push survivors off generators by simply slowing down their repair speed and, thus, increasing their overall repair time. This, with enough buildup, is enough to shift the momentum back in Clown’s favor since that disproportionate imbalance of generator repair speeds vs. hook times now becomes positive. The agency that survivors have becomes fruitless as their actions don’t achieve any lasting results.
Your main heavy-hitting perks are Dying Light , Scourge Hook: Gift of Pain , and Surge/Jolt . Dying Light and Gift of Pain are extremely threatening when used together. Their repair slowdown not only stacks with each other, but Clown has nearly full control over when survivors lose Dying Light or Gift of Pain. As long as Clown does not injure a survivor, they will not lose their Gift of Pain repair speed debuff. Similarly, Dying Light only deactivates after the Obsession has been sacrificed. As long as the Obsession is still alive, then Dying Light’s global repair speed slowdown will be active. This slowdown alongside Surge/Jolt enables Clown when using this build to focus purely on seeking non-Obsession survivors to both apply Gift of Pain onto them in addition to building stacks of Dying Light. Kicking generators wastes a lot of time and can give survivors outside of your current area free generator progress, as you are not actively threatening them. Surge/Jolt lets you regress immediately and passively without costing you time kicking generators instead of seeking survivors and starting chases. This in essence gives you greater momentum when transitioning between hooking a survivor and your next target.
This is a non-tunneling build. I want to be very, very clear on that. Tunneling with this build is very bad . If a survivor has Decisive Strike , tunneling can really hurt you when playing out the rest of that match. Transferring the Obsession to a survivor who may be on death hook can delay when you need to kill your first survivor, as choosing to kill a survivor who used Decisive Strike and is now the Obsession will deactivate your Dying Light. You want to focus on non-Obsession survivors who you know won’t have any anti-tunnel perks. Do not worry too much at all about unhooked survivors inflicted with the Gift of Pain debuff. They may be healed and working on generators, but the speed penalty is so severe. At even just 1 stack of Dying Light with Gift of Pain active, a solo survivor needs 111 seconds to repair a generator from 0% to completion. Once you’ve spread that debuff to all non-Obsession survivors, you’ve basically locked in immense slowdown that will make it hard for survivors to overcome the efficiency of your chases.
Another important tip with this build is you don’t want to just spread injuries across survivors who have the Gift of Pain debuff if you can help it. Injuring a survivor with the Gift of Pain debuff removes it – so you’re actually giving survivors faster repair speeds – especially if they stay injured while they repair generators since you’re actively focused on their teammates instead of them. You want to commit to downing survivors when injuring a survivor affected by Gift of Pain. That way, you can re-hook them on a Scourge Hook and reset their debuff before you spend some time going after someone else.
One should also remember that Gift of Pain debuffs survivors with both Mangled + Hemorrhage and a healing speed penalty alongside its repair speed effects. When given the opportunity to proxy a recent unhook, one can really hurt survivors by forcing them to split into different directions and regressing all of their healing progress through Hemorrhage. In this situation, one should chase the unhooker so that the unhooked survivor is encouraged to seek out a teammate and pull them off generators in order to heal them. This results in greater overall macro pressure – especially given that said heal accompanies Gift of Pain’s repair speed penalty.
In the event that survivors decide to stay injured in order to avoid the repair speed penalty of Gift of Pain, this is a risk that has the potential to reward you greatly if you can continue succeeding in your chases before you stumble across them, again. Finishing a two-health-state chase and transitioning from there into another very quick chase and down due to said survivor starting the chase injured is a massive swing in tempo not just due to the overall survivor statuses but also because of the rapidly building stacks of Dying Light. Once one reaches Critical Mass with this build – aka, 3 survivors with Gift of Pain and 3 stacks of Dying Light or more, it becomes borderline impossible for the majority of teams to come back against this build. The repair speed vs. hook time proportionality due to the slowdown from Gift of Pain and Dying Light becomes so much in Clown’s favor that its insurmountable even if Clown starts making misplays.
For your 4th perk, the top recommendations I would offer are Play With Your Food , Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , and Scourge Hook: Jagged Compass .
Play With Your Food may seem unorthodox and out of place, but it fits into the build surprisingly well in excellent fashion. It is one of the few perks available that has an influence on reducing pre-running by enabling Clown to close distance faster between himself and a survivor when crossing the map and chaining tiles. It also provides Clown with a necessary consolation in the event he wanders into an area of the map that only has the Obsession. Hooking the Obsession, especially early, seldom provides value for Clown when using this build. With Play With Your Food, one can use the game mechanic of ‘entering chases’ in order to gain stacks before seeking out other survivors. One only needs a single stack of Play With Your Food in order to gain enough of a benefit from it mid-chase to attain a fast hook. I would not suggest overly committing toward gaining stacks of Play With Your Food over chasing non-Obsession survivors. It is purely opportunistic and also serves to discourage the Obsession from interacting with you – as it might just result in them making it easier for you to kill their teammates.
Other perk choices are the Scourge Hook perks Pain Resonance and Jagged Compass . Pain Resonance is more risky, but has the greatest potential benefit when compared to Jagged Compass. The 20% instant regression from Pain Resonance is magnified by the repair speed slowdown of Dying Light and Gift of Pain. It is also more likely to activate at a good time before important generators are completed due to said repair speed slowdown. This instant burst of regression can be the deciding factor in Clown’s ability to prevent two or more grouped survivors from finishing a generator because of Clown’s decision to chase a different survivor. It can also swing even more momentum in Clown’s favor if he returns to the generator and finds a non-Obsession nearby who has been hooked recently plus caught out of position. Keep in mind, though, that one is pretty dependent on Scourge Hook placement when using Pain Resonance in this build. Clown can thankfully use his yellow bottles to make carrying survivors to Scourge Hooks easier, but that only accomplishes so much in so many situations.
If you’re not as concerned with a greater peak in potential, then Jagged Compass is what I would recommend as your 4th perk if you don’t want to take Play With Your Food since it streamlines the build by giving it impeccable consistency. What’s special about Jagged Compass is that every unhooked survivor on a regular hook turns that hook into a Scourge Hook and applies Gift of Pain. This basically removes the Scourge Hook requirement and allows you to choose hooks primarily based on their positioning instead of their status for activating other perks within your build. It also has the benefit of highlighting the generator with the most repair progress if you hook a survivor onto a hook which was a Scourge Hook beforehand. With this information, Clown can make the most use of his time given his poor map mobility by pressuring the areas of the map with the most repair progress.

Following the recent buff to Grim Embrace , it is now possible to run this perk alongside both Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance and Dead Man’s Switch in order to get some really strong global synergy. If one can secure at least one kill while there are still stacks of Grim Embrace and Pain Resonance remaining, then those proceeding applications of both perks will show themselves to be absolutely game changing. Coup de Grace is probably Clown’s strongest and most consistent chase perk in the entire game. With practice, one can dramatically shorten their chase times beyond what would ever be possible without it. Coup de Grace is not only amazing for tunneling, but it’s also fantastic for slugging if you happen to be sitting on a lot of stacks. Reliable Early-Mid Stall w/ Tunnel Protection
Perks: Coup de Grace, Forced Penance (flex), Corrupt Intervention, Deadlock
Add-ons: Cigar Box , Garish Makeup Kit
Unlike many of the other builds that I have recommended within this guide, this is one of the first that does not voluntarily utilize Zanshin Tactics for the Mandatory perk slot. Although Zanshin Tactics provides exceptional information that can be used to drastically increase your momentum in chase, that information has a hard limit that is defined by the extent of Clown’s base lethality. This is far different from Coup de Grace , which increases Clown’s lethality beyond its original limit. In essence, when comparing Zanshin Tactics to Coup de Grace , it is a question of Momentum vs . Lethality . Due to the purpose of this build, you’re going to want all the lethality you can get. Any differential loss of momentum can be partially compensated both by Coup de Grace itself as well as Cigar Box + Garish Makeup Kit .
The reason for each of these perks is that this build is designed around extremely aggressive tunneling. You want to deny any early generator progress at the start using Corrupt Intervention so that you can get your first hook. From there, you want to relentlessly pursue the person off the hook and continuously hook them until they are out of the match. Survivors will be doing generators in the background, but you have perks specifically designed around taking advantage of when that happens. Whenever a generator is completed, both Deadlock and Coup de Grace will give you some edge in ensuring that you can successfully down again whoever it is you are tunneling before more generators are completed. Once a survivor is out of the match, the overall pressure of survivors when it comes to completing your generators becomes significantly smaller. It also makes their individual mistakes more punishing – thus opening up possibilities to snowball and win the match outright at once.
You’re likely going to have some survivors show up to bodyblock for the person that you’re tunneling in your matches. That is why the build has Forced Penance . Whenever a survivor bodyblocks against Forced Penance, they are going to be Broken for so long that they can’t bodyblock for that survivor again. Both the Broken status effect as well as its duration from Forced Penance are incredibly powerful. A survivor that is Broken cannot fully heal by any means until the debuff expires. The only relative counter that exists for Forced Penance is Vigil in order to shorten the overall duration. Survivors that show up to bodyblock for a survivor on death hook are especially vulnerable to Forced Penance, as you can immediately start a chase with them after sacrificing another survivor while they are still Broken.
Assuming survivors don’t show up to bodyblock for whoever you’re tunneling, the value of Forced Penance as a perk slot is not entirely wasted when you consider that this means no one is providing support for the person you are tunneling. If no one ever shows up to bodyblock, tunneling out a survivor off the hook is always going to be drastically easier. Any tunneled survivor who cannot perform a strong chase while also possessing multiple layers of anti-tunnel perks is not going to last long enough for their team without the protection of other survivors. Worst case scenario, Deadlock will buy Clown some extra time and Coup de Grace will enable him to get hits on the tunneled survivor that are borderline uncounterable.
It should be heavily emphasized that this build has the potential to be absolutely crippling against unprepared teams where none of the survivors have any anti-tunnel perks. Basekit BT alone is not going to save survivors that are hooked within dead zones or other dangerous locations with unsafe loops – especially when Coup de Grace is in play. Other survivor teammates are basically required to show up and stop you from tunneling out the person off the hook in order to prevent it from happening (Forced Penance value). Whenever this doesn’t take place, it won’t be uncommon for you to have matches where the first survivor is dead as early as 3 or 4 generators remaining.
I highly recommend this build if you are already decently-capable of executing yellow + pink bottle combos around pre-dropped pallets without Coup de Grace actually being in play. If you can go so far as to get these hits in regularly without even lunging , then you will be in a fantastic spot when it comes to getting the most value out of this build. At that level of skill, Coup de Grace becomes purely an extension of your overall capability and lets you attain heights that would otherwise be impossible to reach without it.
‘Plan B’ Clown
Perks: Zanshin Tactics , Rancor (Flex), Corrupt Intervention, Deadlock
Add-ons: Cigar Box , Cheap Gin Bottle ( Otherwise, any other strong add-on combo works)
Even my roughest matches are still salvageable enough with this build to prevent a loss. No matter the circumstances, I can almost always rely on Deadlock to buy me enough time to tunnel at least one person out. From that point, I just need to find the obsession in the post-gen phase and a loss can be prevented due to Rancor . It may not be a win, but it’s a dramatically different outcome compared to other losing matches in the post-gen phase where I would otherwise be camping a hook and going for hook grabs. With this build, it’s unlikely that you should ever be camping hooks in the post-gen phase because you should be spending that time chasing the obsession. It’s a nice quality of life change that also makes the post-gen phase less troublesome.
That is also just the worst-case scenario. It shouldn’t be uncommon at all for you to successfully tunnel two or more non-obsession survivors out of the match before all 5 generators are completed. Once that happens, it doesn’t matter how well the other two survivors play since you will have so many different angles to secure at least three kills no matter how well the survivors perform in chase or how efficiently they do the remaining generators. Actually losing with this build requires a fairly decent number of mistakes on your end coupled with many other external factors such as poor map choice, strong survivor perks/items, gen efficiency, or voice comm coordination and likewise shouldn’t happen very often.
On its own, Deadlock blocks a single generator at a time for a total of 120 seconds. That is plenty of time overall to ensure that you can close out multiple chases before the post-gen phase begins. This is also a slowdown that is applied on any generator regardless of distance and does not require any direct interaction. Given that Clown has very poor map mobility, I’m honestly starting to question whether or not there is any other slowdown perk that is better. Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance used to be my favorite slowdown/regression perk for a long while. However, I’ve been finding it falls out of favor just a bit by comparison given that it is hook-dependent whereas Deadlock isn’t . Hook-dependent perks do not help you very much when you are behind, and the same could be said for Pain Resonance – especially if you get bad scourge hook placement.
One thing to note about this build is that it slightly alters the template by recognizing Rancor as a primary slowdown perk instead of a flex perk. The reason for doing so is that with this specific build I believe it is fair to consider the strength of Rancor as being on par with a slowdown perk if the other slowdown perk is Deadlock. I believe that Deadlock and Rancor have a very strong and interesting synergy that is mostly present on account of how strong and effective tunneling is as a strategy. These two perks together enable Clown to commit more to necessary chases by removing any disincentive for refusing to cross one side of the map or chase multiple survivors. In turn, this means that Clown’s downside of poor map mobility is less prevalent and the gen-efficiency of strong survivors that you cannot counter is less punishing. Without Deadlock, there likely wouldn’t be a good replacement for Rancor to be as effective since there really isn’t any other perk that is as strong or flexible when you need it.
Looking at flex perks, I currently prefer Sloppy Butcher for a few reasons. Mangled on its own can be effective, but mangled and hemorrhage together is even more powerful. Unhooked survivors requiring more time to heal means that you aren’t as dependent on being as close when an unhook takes place. Assuming you can interrupt the unhooked survivor as they are being healed, all of that healing progress they have gained will have been lost. Further, you might have caught them out in a bad position where re-downing and re-hooking them would be easy.
Some people might say that they would prefer Save the Best for Last instead of Sloppy Butcher as a flex perk, but I’m just skeptical on whether or not it would be as effective with this specific build. You likely won’t be hitting 5-6 stacks of STBFL when utilizing an aggressive tunneling playstyle until after one survivor is already dead. While it would be good at gaining some extra lethality as a consolation for whenever other survivors show up to bodyblock in chase, this is only actually true if one of those survivors is never the obsession. If one of the people bodyblocking for whoever you’re chasing is the obsession, then you’re going to be throwing away those stacks instead of gaining them. Sloppy Butcher does not have this problem as it affects all survivors equally no matter their obsession status. It also slows down bodyblocking survivors by increasing the amount of time they need to spend healing before they can bodyblock again. Some survivors will even see this after taking a hit and will opt for doing generators while injured in order to make up for lost efficiency. In turn, you can punish these survivors if you catch them out afterward with a much shorter chase.
If I were to consider swapping out Sloppy Butcher for a different flex perk, I would probably consider swapping it out for Franklin’s Demise . Sloppy Butcher’s biggest weakness has to be medkits because even with the reduced healing speed medkits are just too efficient. Instead of keeping two survivors busy for longer by requiring one survivor to heal the other, a medkit allows one survivor to heal on their own without distracting another survivor from whatever it is that they are doing. This is the same both with and without Sloppy Butcher. In order to have a greater impact against teams with multiple medkits, I’ve found that Franklin’s Demise does a better job by displacing survivors you hit from their items at the location of the action. This prevents survivors from bodyblocking for a teammate and running off to heal as they will no longer have their medkit with them in order to do so. They will have to return to where their medkit is previously located both in order to prevent it from losing charges and also to regain their method of healing themselves.
Instant Regression Snowball Build
Perk s: Zanshin Tactics , Save the Best for Last (Flex) , Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Pop Goes the Weasel
What I really like about this build is that it provides a significant amount of pressure that only keeps getting better as you continue to do well in chase. Pain Resonance and PGTW have incredible synergy due to survivors usually choosing to tap the Pain Ress’d generator. If you can figure out which generator got hit with Pain Resonance, you can follow up on that generator with PGTW for an even greater combined instant regression penalty. It becomes a lot harder for survivors to prioritize one or two specific generators when Pain Resonance also informs you of which generator has the most progress. You are able to use this information to ensure that whichever generator you kick is going to get the most value out of PGTW that is possible.
I choose to put Save the Best for Last in this build as my flex perk because it allows me to become even stronger as I’m successfully ending chases and downing survivors. Being able to end your chases faster enables more situations where you can prevent generators from being completed using Pain Resonance and PGTW. It also has the benefit of late game utility in the event that you have to camp any hooks. The increased weapon recovery speed means that you can recover fast enough between weapon swings to down a survivor before an unhook is completed.
Keep in mind that choosing to run STBFL opens you up to massive counterplay by the obsession. You can’t afford to chase them nor can you afford to hit them in order to push them away due to how much effectiveness it takes away from the perk. Even just injuring the obsession once means you now need the equivalent of a hook just to recoup those lost stacks.
Roaming Chase w/ Instant Regression
Perks: Coup de Grace, Unrelenting, Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance, Pop Goes the Weasel
Pop Goes the Weasel and Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance was already a decent instant regression combo before the basekit buff to generator kicking. Although Clown lacks in map mobility, applying PGTW is not that difficult when you have Pain Resonance to stall so that Clown can roam. One can either try to seek the generator that was Pain Ress’d or any other generator with considerable progress. Assuming one can actually apply PGTW to a generator that is near completion but not fully repaired, the momentum swing can buy Clown enough time to rotate back toward his hooked survivor so that they can be tunneled summarily afterward.
It’s important to keep in mind that this build lacks Corrupt Intervention for the early stages of the game. You not only need to find a survivor quickly, but you also have to down them quickly, as well. If it takes you anywhere from 15-20 seconds just to find a survivor, that chase needs to end in under 30 seconds in order prevent either two survivors working together or one survivor with a toolbox from finishing a generator during your first chase. Thankfully, Unrelenting is available immediately at the start of your matches and can secure hits that survivors either don’t expect or are otherwise unprepared to go against. Once you can get that momentum going, Coup de Grace should be all the lethality Clown needs from his perks in order to secure fast hits and downs in tandem with his power.
Another great tip with this build is that one can use Clown’s yellow bottles when carrying a survivor in order to hook aggressively. Rather than hook a survivor in a corner away from the rest of the team, Clown can hook said survivor nearby a generator with high repair progress and proxy the hook. PGTW will not only remove a lot of repair progress from that generator, but Pain Resonance (if available) can also get applied to a different generator at another location.
Maximum Global Presence Slowdown Build
Perks : Zanshin Tactics , Flex Perk, Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Deadlock
Add-ons : Nothing specific - Refer to the Tier List
Before I began seriously testing this build, I had concerns that there would be too much perk anti-synergy. Running Deadlock and Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance together leaves you vulnerable in the event that a generator is completed shortly before you are able to hook a survivor that you recently downed in chase. While this is true, I have determined through my own matches that both the gravity and prevalence of this downside is not negated by the strengths that each of these individual slowdown perks provide across the board.
When you look beyond this one vulnerable period, both Pain Resonance and Deadlock actually synergize incredibly well. Clown’s biggest weakness is his poor map mobility. Even with his strong chases, his poor map mobility means that against efficient survivors Clown can’t get value out of many hook-dependent regression or slowdown perks. Pain Resonance is probably one of the few perks that Clown is able to get full consistent value out of its instant regression on account of the fact that Pain Resonance will hit the generator with the most progress no matter where it is on the map. Similarly, Deadlock also has no range limitation and will apply to a generator with that same condition. This means that your perk build is going to be less hindered by map choice countering its downsides and, thus, your matches should also be more consistent.
It should be mentioned that there are also going to be times where you would want Deadlock to block a generator during that vulnerable period I previously mentioned. Strong and efficient survivors like to spread their pressure across multiple generators. Even if you can’t get value out of Pain Resonance due to Deadlock blocking the generator, you can at the very least use the white aura around the generator to establish information. Further, the slowdown gained by Deadlock exceeds anything you would have gained from Pain Resonance as there would be nothing stopping one or two survivors from finishing a nearly completed generator shortly after. With Deadlock, there is literally nothing any survivor can do for 30 seconds to that one generator. In a way, this is effectively a generator-specific incapacitation applied to the whole team, again, with zero range limitations.
After trying out a variety of flex perks, I do think that this build is open to plenty of creativity. You can run Save the Best for Last to give yourself better lethality, Sloppy Butcher to punish bodyblocking and give you a larger window for proxy tunneling, Franklin’s Demise in case survivors bring three medkits, Eruption for even more slowdown, and more. The main strengths to the build when it comes to extending how much time you have in chase are explicitly Deadlock and Pain Resonance . Beyond that, your flex perk is meant to compliment any certain area or aspect to your gameplay that you think will result in a better performance.
As a closing mention to this build, through my own experience, I have found so far that this one specific template seems to work the best when it comes to encountering extremely high level players. When going against survivors that are incredibly skilled in chase who also have teammates that know how to efficiently repair generators, you genuinely cannot afford to waste any – and I mean ANY – time at all walking across the map. I cannot emphasize this enough. Not only does crossing the map displace you from any pressure you’ve already created on one side, but it also enables survivors to re-establish control over that area before you return. If you overextend away from a hooked survivor, a teammate can unhook that survivor, heal them, and then run away before you can do anything about it due to Clown’s poor map mobility. Further, you risk the chance when initially crossing the map to pressure a generator for that generator to be completed before you even have a chance to get there.
Against this caliber of survivors, you need to hedge your strengths to the maximum and take full advantage of what Pain Resonance and Deadlock are capable of doing to make up for your downsides. You may not be able to cross the map to pressure a Deadlocked generator, but you can use the time that a generator is Deadlocked to tunnel the survivor with the most hook pressure. Similarly, while Pain Resonance is only 13.5 seconds of regression for a single survivor (7 seconds for two), you save the time you would have otherwise wasted crossing the map to apply that regression and can also be more efficient in pressure without overextending as far away from whoever you have hooked.
Triple Instant Regression Slowdown Build
Perks: Zanshin Tactics , Eruption (Flex) , Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Pop Goes the Weasel
Add-ons: Cigar Box (Highly recommended), Cheap Gin Bottle (Best, but others work fine)
This is a good build if you feel like the two slowdown perk slots aren’t enough to stop generators from being completed. Every single one of these regression perks synergizes with the other to some reasonable degree. You don’t need to spend a lot of time kicking generators at the start of the match to ensure that you get Eruption value since Eruption is only your flex regression .
Your primary regression tools are Pain Resonance and Pop Goes the Weasel . These two perks enable you to immediately seek out a chase as a match begins due to the combined value you gain from hooking a survivor. Pain Resonance will apply a significant instant regression penalty to the most progressed generator . If you can identify this generator, you can very likely kick it afterward with PGTW and ensure that you get the most value possible from the perk. Most survivors will at least stay long enough to tap a Pain Ress’d generator if they are going to leave so that it doesn’t passively regress. This gives you your opportunity to use PGTW . Further, you should also be able to inflict Eruption on that generator you kick with PGTW since it should be off cooldown by the time you kick it. Eruption’s cooldown takes effect when you down a survivor. Unless you would be needing to kick a generator that is right next to where you downed a survivor, thirty seconds should otherwise expire by the time you recover from the successful M1 animation, pick that survivor up, carry them to a hook, and walk across the map.
Although this build is packed with a heavy amount of generator regression, it lacks any type of survivor tracking while also being entirely dependent on you performing well in chases. If you run this build and you aren’t downing survivors, you won’t be getting value from any out of your regression perks. You will be essentially perkless outside of Zanshin Tactics . Survivors will punish this by completing your generators at a speed that you cannot keep up with. Running Cigar Box is highly recommended for this reason (assuming you aren’t skilled with Pinkie Finger) since it both provides a little bit of survivor tracking in addition to streamlining your chases at high-wall tiles. Sometimes, it’s difficult to track survivors based on sound alone when you’re in the middle of a chase. Cigar Box increases your lethality by removing your dependence on sound and allowing you to focus purely on sight. This removes the mind-game factor at high-wall tiles and allows you to act based on information that survivors do not know you have. With this information, you should be able to hit survivors in most practical situations assuming you properly set up your double-bottle combos.
0 Gen-Kicking Instant Regression Build w/ Lethal
Perks: Zanshin Tactics , Lethal Pursuer (Flex) , Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Jolt
Add-ons: Nothing specific - Refer to the Tier List
This is one build possibility you could consider if you really don’t want to kick generators. Pain Resonance and Jolt synergize together very well and provide a potential for 23% instant regression on one generator. This regression is also fully applied by the time you hook a survivor – no downtime is necessary. Lethal Pursuer doesn’t play any major integral within the build; you could probably replace it with a different one if you wanted. Its main purpose is ensuring that you pick the smartest first chase possible in order to minimize how much generator progress survivors can do. Not only does Lethal let you break up two survivors who start a match working on the same generator, but it also helps you identify generator clusters for increased Jolt value when you pick your first chase.
With this convenience sadly does come some heavy downsides. If you’re only able to consistently hit one generator with Jolt every time you down a survivor, then the regression you gain from it alone likely doesn’t warrant the perk slot. You are going to be completely reliant on getting Pain Resonance to trigger so that you can regress whichever generators survivors are actually repairing. Larger maps can punish you for this since you may lack the map mobility to pressure survivors repairing a Pain Ress’d generator before it is completed.
I would mainly only consider running a build like this if I were bringing specific map offerings. Although Jolt can be pretty weak on larger maps, it is absolutely incredible on smaller maps. Gideon Meat Plant, Midwich Elementary School, and Dead Dawg Saloon are all maps where this build has the potential to earn you excellent returns from your perk choices. Lethal is also much less necessary on these maps compared to others due to their size. Zanshin Tactics can even be swapped out for a different perk assuming you send yourself to Midwich. I would still keep Zanshin for Gideon due to its pallet density and Dead Dawg Saloon because of the jungle gyms at the bottom half of the map. Midwich Elementary School has virtually very little changes in random map generation every time you play on it. With enough experience, Zanshin becomes unnecessary on Midwich since it becomes possible to learn the properties of almost every single tile spawn.
0 Gen-Kicking Stall Build w/ Late Game Cleanup
Perks: Zanshin Tactics , Hex: No One Escapes Death (Flex) , Corrupt Intervention , Deadlock
This is a build I would only recommend if you’re either still learning Clown or are having problems consistently ending your chases quickly. It has zero instant regression, zero increased passive regression, creates zero pressure during the generator-phase whenever you down/hook a survivor, and possesses very limited snowball potential. In turn, it’s probably not a build you might want to consider if you’re playing very well.
Where this build excels is slowing down survivors that are already in control of the match against you. Corrupt Intervention stops survivors from immediately hopping on generators and finishing either 1-2 of them before you finish your first chase. Getting at least one hook on a survivor before your first generator pops is the bare minimum pressure you need in order to have a chance at winning a match. Anything less than that, and you’re likely to struggle killing just two survivors before the generators are completed. The initial momentum created from those completed generators is just too great when you’re also playing against survivors that know how to capitalize off of it. CI gives you a better chance of stopping survivors from creating this initial momentum.
Similarly, the same can also be said for Deadlock . When you’re underperforming or survivors are overperforming, Deadlock is probably the best slowdown perk in the entire game. It has global presence, prioritizes the strongest generator, and has very little counterplay outside of high-level SWF coordination. You can rely on Deadlock either offensively or defensively when you’re struggling in a match to ensure that you have enough time to close out a key chase before survivors finish more generators.
While you could probably swap out the flex perk with something else, I felt like Hex: No One Escapes Death has the best synergy on account of the purpose behind running this build. The post-gen phase is the shortest period of the entire match and also the one period where survivors have the most agency to assist one another in chase. When you are behind on kills, you cannot afford to chase one survivor during the post-gen phase for an extended period of time. Bodyblocks and remaining resources coupled with the injury speed boost makes closing out these chases too difficult. NOED enables you to circumvent those problems by giving you the ability to down survivors in one hit. If you can quickly find a survivor on death hook during the post-gen phase, you can use the surprise factor of NOED to possibly catch out a second survivor before the other survivors can escape. Assuming that the second survivor was also on death hook, you can literally swing a tie-outcome near the end of the match from just this one perk alone.
Heavy Totem Slowdown Build w/ some Instant Regression
Perks: Zanshin Tactics , Hex: Plaything ( Necessary Flex) , Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Hex: Pentimento
This build slightly breaks the mold a bit when it comes to the Optimal Build Template. Unlike the other perk builds in this guide, this one has a necessary flex perk – which means it is hardly a flex perk at all. Hex: Plaything is absolutely mandatory assuming you want to run Hex: Pentimento since there is no other way to persistently encourage survivors to cleanse totems. Running a singular hex like Devour Hope or Blood Favour is only going to get you one Pentimento totem against smart survivors at the cost of two perk slots. At that point, you might as well run two different perks besides either Plaything or Pentimento.
Sometimes you will get survivors who won’t cleanse the Plaything hexes, but you can use this to your advantage by stealthing up to them from an angle they didn’t expect. If you can punish their choice not to cleanse Plaything with another hook state, that’s still really good value even without Pentimento’s slowdown. Further, Pain Resonance can still activate and provide you with at least some regression to reward you for your time spent in chase. This is compounded further since that survivor you hooked again will now either be dead or one hook away from sacrifice.
If they do cleanse Plaything hexes, that means they’re spending a lot of time without repairing generators. You can then Pentimento those totems and encourage them to spend even more time cleansing hexes. Survivors that sit on generators while Pentimento is active are how this build becomes most effective. As long as you’re successfully downing survivors in a reasonable amount of time, the combined slowdown + regression of Pentimento and Pain Resonance is very notic eable. For one survivor, 15% regression with Pentimento is equivalent to 19.28 seconds-worth of progress. You should be able to slow down the match long enough to get two kills before the generators are done if your chases are fast enough. From there, you just need to clean up by finding one of the two remaining survivors before they escape through the exit gates.
The major downside with this build is that it does nothing at all to slow down survivors if they do not cleanse their Plaything hexes and you aren’t able to perform well in chase. Pain Resonance requires you to keep downing survivors in order to hook them so that you can regress the generators. If you’re having to cut chases early without downing a survivor so that you can cross the map and stop a generator from being completed, this build has no perks in play that are preventing that from happening. You will be essentially playing the match from behind perkless outside of Zanshin Tactics .

Non-Meta Perk Builds
I frequently get a lot of questions about certain perks, perk combinations, and perk builds that are really interesting but, sadly, not ‘viable’ on Clown in the current meta. This is because they either lack consistency or the desired effect isn’t meaningful enough to create game-winning pressure . A Clown build fully designed around slugging, for example, falls apart on a large map against a survivor team in comms that can simultaneously coordinate safe pick-ups and efficient generator progress. However, that very same build might absolutely destroy a team of solo-queue survivors should you happen to bring a map offering or get sent to a very small map.
Out of interest for my viewers and readers who are less concerned with optimizing their kill rates and play Dead by Daylight more casually, I will spend some time below in the following section discussing some unique build possibilities for those seeking something more unconventional.

Swiss-Army Knife Clown
Perks: Coup de Grace, Unrelenting, Zanshin Tactics, Superior Anatomy/Sloppy Butcher
Add-ons: Cigar Box , Garish Makeup Kit/Sulfuric Acid Vial (Don’t take w/ Sloppy Butcher)
The name of this build comes from the fact that each perk has its own independent purpose for helping out Clown in chase. When you combine all of these perks together, Clown becomes equipped to tackle a variety of situations across multiple different tiles that he would be otherwise unprepared to handle. This build requires one to have good game sense and a smart understanding of where they should be at any given moment. Downtime has to be kept to a minimum since this build is fully designed around making Clown’s chases faster and, consequently, it does not possess any generator stall or slowdown.
Despite this flaw, what’s most appealing about using this perk build is you will feel extremely comfortable chasing survivors regardless of what map is chosen. Zanshin Tactics will give you an outline of how to preemptively place Clown’s bottles once you have experience, Coup de Grace / Unrelenting both will give you extra general lethality, and Superior Anatomy means that even maps like Garden of Joy or Haddonfield no longer have the threat of their strong main building windows. You will encounter few situations running this build where you don’t feel like you have a tool to help you hit a survivor when you need it.
While Superior Anatomy can definitely be helpful on certain maps like Garden of Joy or Haddonfield, it is rather niche due to how infrequently one might end up on these maps (let alone engage in a chase where it provides said value). For this reason, I would recommend Sloppy Butcher in its place as an alternative. Being able to either keep or encourage survivors to stay injured creates future snowball opportunities once you’ve hooked a different survivor. Injured survivors cannot afford to make a single mistake in chase while still having to contend with Clown’s bottles and three other chase perks. Coup de Grace especially becomes more dangerous if multiple survivors decide to remain injured. 1 completed generator can translate into as much as 2 hooks if both stacks are used in chase against injured survivors. Slugging opportunistically is similarly another strategy that becomes viable for this same reason.
Casino Clown
Perks: Coup de Grace, Hex: Blood Favor, Hex: Haunted Grounds, Hex: Retribution
This build is somewhat gimmicky, but it has the potential to be really oppressive if the gambling odds decide to work out in your favor. How the hexes work together is basically like a game of Chance . If survivors cleanse the right hexes, minimal bad things happen. If they cleanse the wrong hexes, pandemonium has a chance of breaking loose. With Coup de Grace being included to increase Clown’s lunge range, every hex chosen also becomes even stronger .
Together, these 3 hex perks spawn a total of 4 hex totems. Unlike Blood Favor or Retribution , Haunted Grounds creates 2 hex totems instead of 1 . This means that the odds of a survivor cleansing Haunted Grounds first instead of either Blood Favor or Retribution is doubled at 50 %. Haunted Grounds getting cleansed first is the most ideal outcome and what will create for you the most pressure. If Haunted Grounds is cleansed first, Retribution will activate and show you the location of every exposed survivor for 15 seconds. Depending on one’s current number of Coup de Grace stacks, one could opt to slug while Haunted Grounds is still active and secure multiple hooks from a single cleanse – especially with Blood Favor blocking nearby pallets.
Survivors can’t simply avoid cleansing totems because of Blood Favor’s ever-present threat. If a survivor gets hit before they can make it to a pallet while Blood Favor is still active, it is almost guaranteed that they will be downed shortly afterward. The odds of Blood Favor getting cleansed first is only 25%, so one is unlikely to lose Blood Favor while they have both Haunted Grounds or Retribution still active. However, this can be extremely crippling to your performance with this build in the matches where it does happen – so keep that in mind.
Losing Retribution first instead of either Blood Favor or Haunted Grounds is also a bad outcome, but it’s not nearly as bad as losing your Blood Favor. Without Retribution, Haunted Grounds won’t provide one with the early global tracking to both find exposed survivors as well as make the best macro decision on who to chase and whether or not to slug. One can still salvage some pressure off of Haunted Grounds by itself, but it’s definitely not ideal.
Anti-Heal Clown
Perks: Coup de Grace, Sloppy Butcher, Coulrophobia, Unnerving Presence/Infect. Fright
This build is fantastic for keeping survivors injured assuming you’re able to reliably split your pressure. Both Sloppy Butcher and Coulrophobia when combined together reduce healing speeds so massively that it almost becomes a complete waste of time to even bother. However, the decision to voluntarily stay injured against Clown is also very dangerous – especially when Clown has Coup de Grace . An injured survivor who is unprepared against a skilled Clown with Coup de Grace is very likely to find themselves downed within 15 seconds. All it takes is a single double-bottle combo around a pre-dropped pallet and then Clown earns himself another hook.
Keep in mind that this build lacks any and all forms of generator regression or slowdown. Any generator repair progress the survivors earn is likely going to stick. As Clown, one has to play from the start of the match with this build under the mindset that they are racing against time. If one cannot snowball off of the split pressure created by Sloppy Butcher and Coulrophobia, then there’s nothing left in this specific build that can enable Clown to win.
A very good tip if you are going to run this build is to take a horizontally-small map offering. Dead Dawg Saloon , Gideon Meat Plant , and Midwich Elementary School are all great maps for this build since it’s both easier to keep survivors within your terror radius as well as roam the map. You want to be catching survivors while they are stuck in place healing each other and then interrupt their progress. If you either don’t have or don’t bring one of these map offerings, you run the risk of accidentally ending up on a map like Mother’s Dwelling or Temple of Purgation and not able to get value out of your perks.
Dissolution Chase Build
Perks: Save the Best for Last, Rapid Brutality, Dissolution, Superior Anatomy
It’s normally hard to get value out of Dissolution on Clown unless you provide it with some support. All a survivor has to do is run in the opposite direction from Clown after getting injured in order to wait out the timer. Save the Best for Last and Rapid Brutality are wonderful compliments that will prevent survivors from ignoring Dissolution so easily. Survivors won’t be able to safely chain as many tiles before Clown is finally close again. In turn, as the value of Dissolution becomes more consistent, it as a whole also becomes a lot more practical.
One should avoid the Obsession as frequently as possible with this build. Lost STBFL stacks penalizes Clown significantly since it weakens the overall lethality of Dissolution. Sometimes it may be required to injure the obsession (such as if they are bodyblocking), but one should always try to avoid it unless absolutely necessary. Clown cannot afford to lose ramping pressure by hitting the Obsession with reckless abandon.
Superior Anatomy is chosen as the last perk since Dissolution, Save the Best for Last, and Rapid Brutality should be able to handle pallet loops. I’ve always been partial to Superior Anatomy over Bamboozle because it’s usually unlikely as Clown that one will loop back to the same window more than one time. Other perks like Coup de Grace would definitely be great, but probably not nearly as necessary due to how much pallet control the build already has. Sloppy Butcher could also be a good choice in the event that one is able to spread around a little pressure. The reduced healing speeds can potentially create snowball opportunities where Clown is able to start and quickly end chases with injured survivors.
Remember, of course, that this build does not have any generator regression or slowdown. One needs to play fully committed to the idea of going for chases with this build, as there’s nothing in your build stopping multiple survivors from splitting up across your generators and winning through pure meta efficiency. Any generator progress survivors earn is going to stick.
Ascending Clown’s Limitations and Activating the Gate of Death
“It is the climax of youth when it burns most crimson!”
Background
For the longest time, Clown has struggled with being appreciated for his strengths rather than disliked for his weaknesses by those who play him. Even though since his rework Clown has been capable of faster chase times than the majority of every other killer on the roster, many average to high-level survivors have recognized that the best way to beat Clown is to not interact with him and repair generators as quickly as possible. This had typically led to very fast matches, as Clown’s chase times – as fast as they were  – were still not quick enough in order to compete with the speed that generators were being completed. Perks like Corrupt Intervention and Deadlock were borderline necessary against some of the stronger teams not just to establish early pressure but also to maintain it. Pressure with Clown can be very volatile. Mistakes in micro gameplay are felt by the efficiency of every other survivor not actively engaged with you in chase.
These factors are typically what lead to people eventually dropping Clown for another killer. No map pressure/mobility means that you have to be on point in your chases in order to have a chance at killing three skilled survivors or more in one match. Even when you’re playing well, sometimes you may still lose generators due to either sheer efficiency, strong survivor loadouts, a rough map, or all of the above. Some people like to refer to playing Clown as “a whole lot of squeeze without the juice.” Personally, I see it as trying to pressure a diamond out of coal. If you can’t provide enough heat, your chances of winning grind into dust.
Until the 7.2 Patch, I used to think that generating enough heat from chases alone wasn’t possible even for me if the survivors were both skilled and coordinated. Map size, pre-running before the chase begins, and being able to chain multiple strong tiles together are usually all survivors need in order to last long enough for their team at the bare minimum. That’s not including weapon recovery animations, the pickup animation, or the time spent having to carry a survivor to a hook after winning a chase, either. All of that downtime adds up when you don’t have any map mobility.
In order to compensate for this glaring weakness, I opted heavily toward extremely aggressive camping and tunneling. By chasing survivors almost immediately as they got unhooked, I could begin a chase with them while they were still injured. Further, I could engineer the chase to be even more in my favor by strategically hooking said survivor in a very dangerous location. I would then equalize the pressure lost from completed generators by using Coup de Grace to tunnel survivors out of the match faster and reduce the team’s overall generator efficiency. Once there are only three survivors remaining, it becomes drastically easier for Clown to keep up with the pacing of the game. Other survivors would realize this and try to prevent me from tunneling their teammates – pulling them off of generators in the process.
While this strategy has worked out for me in the majority of my matches, it has never always been a surefire win. When the survivors are on point, all it takes is one or two mistakes from me mid-chase in order for my best result in that match to potentially become two kills. E ven I ha d found playing Clown to be very stressful at different times as a result. To make matters worse, there was very little positive reciprocation received from many survivors that I went against because I employed these tactics. Survivors would either say nothing at all or they would say something rude. Some would take it as a validation to be increasingly hostile. It made simply wanting to win controversial as a mindset due to the manner that was necessary in order to make it happen.
Following the 7.2 Patch, with the addition of Rapid Brutality , what I have done here is an ambitious attempt to finally break that mold. 1. I want people who play Clown to respect him for what he can do – not look down on him for what he can’t do. 2. I want people to feel like when they play Clown that they have more agency to be in control of the match by using his bottles effectively. 3. Lastly, I want survivors who go against him to finally have a real reason to fear Clown out of recognition of his power.
This build, I believe, accomplishes every single one of those goals.
Getting Started
First things, first: the name of the build – Gate of Death . The name is derived from the anime Naruto Shippuden . During a moment of desperation, one of the characters in the anime powers themselves up to otherwise unreachable heights at the cost of rapidly dying without any means to stop it. The spirit behind that transformation is exactly what I’ve replicated here.
What this build does is play entirely into Clown’s sole strength by focusing exclusively on perks that give Clown greater lethality. Every single perk taken synergizes with Clown’s power as well as with each other. By combining all three of these perks together, Clown can now reach a level of lethality that I would argue rivals even the strongest of killers. As a survivor, not only are singular tiles now borderline impossible to play against because of Coup de Grace and Rapid Brutality , but you can’t even chain tiles as effectively, either, due to Save the Best for Last . This build leaves very few options for survivors to stay alive once a chase has been initiated. Certain death is set to befall any and all who cross your path extremely quickly. You can expect average chase times with this build of 20-25 seconds for two health states – and that’s just the average . With peak performance and execution, it becomes possible to hit the same survivor twice within as little as 10-15 seconds . Yet, there’s still one really important detail about the build that one needs to know.
Per its namesake, the Gate of Death does have one extremely glaring downside that likely makes it really hard to use properly by Clown players who are not skilled enough to handle it. Make no mistake , this is NOT a beginner or intermediate Clown build. With all of your perk slots being invested into lethality, you don’t have anything to slow down generators from being completed beyond basekit kick regression. No Corrupt Intervention means that you can likely expect multiple generators to get worked on right at the start. Without Deadlock , you could have more than one generator get completed at the exact same time and throw off your momentum. Similarly, running zero instant regression perks like Scourge Hook: Pain Resonance , Pop Goes the Weasel , Jolt , or Eruption means that whatever pressure you gain from successfully downing and hooking survivors won’t influence the time it takes for survivors to complete the remaining generators. In order to compensate for lacking in any manner of regression, slowdown, or stall, the Clown player has to do it himself through raw power alone.
Assuming one can’t make proper use of this raw power, they would be better off returning to a standard Template build. Using the Gate of Death build before one is ready can very easily result in one losing that match very quickly . It is as forgiving onto yourself as it is onto others. As strong as you may become when you run this build, that strength isn’t guaranteed to last long if you aren’t practiced or trained to handle it.
How the Build Works
At the beginning of every match, while you may have the most generators remaining, you start the Trial at your weakest point. You do not have any stacks of Save the Best for Last , no stacks of Coup de Grace , and you still have to personally find a survivor on your own volition just to get a chase going. As much emphasis as I’ve put on how powerful this build lets you become, it is this aspect of the build that is going to hold people back the most. One really bad early game performance can potentially result in an unsalvageable match where you just fizzle out.
Finding a survivor and starting your first chase with this build as soon as possible is incredibly important. If it’s the obsession, you can break chase almost immediately to get a stack of Play With Your Food as well as even potentially an early injury beforehand if they’re close enough. That stack of PWYF will help you afterward get your first down when you find a non-obsession survivor. Play With Your Food is basically Rapid Brutality without a timer. You sacrifice a stack of PWYF to injure a survivor and trade it in for Rapid Brutality. From there, Rapid Brutality will help you hit that survivor a second time and get them downed. Every full chase with a non-obsession survivor is 2 stacks of Save the Best for Last . Your goal with this build should be to get as many hooks and stacks of Save the Best for Last as you can before your first generator is finished.
Once your first generator is officially completed, assuming you also have anywhere from 4-6 stacks of Save the Best for Last, you will have everything that you need in order to begin wreaking havoc. With Coup de Grace now usable  if you ever need it, the only obstacles that you will ever find in your path are pallets that block full hallways and strong map-specific window vaults. Just about every single individual tile should now be easily playable – pre-dropped pallet or not. Further, Rapid Brutality and Save the Best for Last will ensure that you’ll be immediately on top of any survivor after hitting them should they try to abandon the tile.
From this point until the end of the match, you should be looking to initiate as many chases as possible as quickly as you can . Be cognizant of your generator locations and use your game sense in order to identify where survivors will be and what they might be doing. This is absolutely necessary in order to keep your momentum when you aren’t running any perks to slow down generator repair speeds. It is explicitly your potential to end chases incredibly fast after you start them that stops generators from being completed.
In order to help others better understand how exactly it is that every single one of these perks operate together alongside Clown’s bottles, I’ve devised a couple of diagrams that should demonstrate the cycling flow of each perk’s benefits across every one of your chases. I call this rotation of synergy the “ Speed Cycle .”
Speed Cycles
Memorize and learn every Speed Cycle. Each of these three examples is how you should expect to play out a grand majority of your chases against healthy survivors . After every M1, Rapid Brutality activates. Depending on your current stacks of Save the Best for Last , your successful attack recovery timer is now also reduced. From there, you can either use Coup de Grace or a pink bottle (if it is needed) before you M1 for the second time and win the chase.
You want to always try for a yellow bottle over a pink bottle, if you can, before M1ing a healthy survivor. Shortly after being injured, survivors are immune to all hindrance effects in addition to gaining a speed boost. This means that your pink bottles will not reduce the distance a survivor gains by their injured speed boost. It also won’t prevent them from doing fast vaults even while intoxicated. Unless you absolutely need a pink bottle to get the first hit before a survivor is able to make it toward an obstacle of safety, throwing a yellow bottle instead (as shown in 2. ) is almost always going to make your chase times faster. When you’re sped up by a yellow bottle while Rapid Brutality is active, you’re essentially moving as if you had Bloodlust III . Factor in some slowdown from a pink bottle in addition to Coup de Grace’s extra lunge range (as shown in 3. ), and you’ve reached close to the pinnacle of lethality.
I say close , because there is still a set of Speed Cycles that is even stronger . This next particular set of Speed Cycles is explicitly reserved for the most desperate of chases due how resource-intensive each of these next Speed Cycles happen to be. They are not efficient. However, sometimes what you need is that extra power in the heat of the moment. For that reason, I call these the “ Overdrive Cycles .”
Overdrive Cycles
The Overdrive Cycles are very similar to regular Speed Cycles in structure. There is only one major difference between them. Unlike Speed Cycles, Overdrive Cycles always start and end using Coup de Grace . This makes their potential far greater than any other combo or setup available at your disposal. Whenever you need to end a chase with a particular survivor as fast as possible, that is what the Overdrive Cycles are for. Usually, this is done when you are chasing a survivor that you know is one hook away from being sacrificed. There is virtually nothing that a survivor can do in order to counter any of the Overdrive Cycles . Against any of the regular Speed Cycles, a survivor can at least attempt to stall the first M1 by pre-running early. Overdrive Cycles make pre-running not even practical as a result of closing so much initial distance by using Coup de Grace for the first M1.
In almost every scenario, Overdrive Cycles 1. and 2. should be all that you need in order to down a priority survivor in chase. However, sometimes there are situations where you need something even stronger. Beyond Overdrive Cycles 1. and 2. is an Overdrive Cycle /bottle combination that I consider to be so powerful it might as well be regarded as the ultimate technique . The influence of every single perk you have in your build on this particular double-bottle combo transcends Clown’s lethality beyond what many would dream could ever be possible. For this reason, I have dubbed the name of this final Overdrive Cycle as the Night Guy Combo . Just like in the anime, this is a penultimate move that expends as much of one’s resources that one has available in order to deliver the most damaging attack that one possibly can. There will seldom ever be any survivor you go against that is prepared to withstand it.
Why Use Play With Your Food?
I want to take some time to address this here, as I feel like some readers might consider this to be either an interesting or controversial pick for a 4th perk. The role of Play With Your Food is purely supportive in nature. The primary significance of your build almost entirely revolves around the synergistic capability resulting from your three other perks. However, without Play With Your Food, it can become a lot harder to maintain stacks of Save the Best for Last whenever the obsession decides to interfere mid-chase. By running Play with Your Food, you can punish the obsession for inserting themselves into your chases by giving yourself even more extra lethality on top of what you already have. It can be game-changing in its own right on account of how it lets you get injuries even faster to activate Rapid Brutality as you commit toward the second M1. Assuming the survivor you initiate a chase with is already injured, it can effectively function as Rapid Brutality during that chase but without a timer.
The sheer power of Play With Your Food can make it extremely obnoxious for the obsession to have any type of involvement with you whatsoever. If they so much as get in your line of sight and enable you to initiate a chase, then they’re screwing over their whole team by giving you even more speed to kill them. However, if they don’t get involved with you, then you’re still going to kill their team since the momentum from 8 stacks of Save the Best for Last, Rapid Brutality, and Coup de Grace is just so absurd that most survivors aren’t ready to handle it. Slugging, especially, is a difficult strategy for the obsession to counter since Clown can keep farming Play With Your Food stacks and use them to slug more and more survivors.
I have tried other perks in place of Play With Your Food – such as Agitation to initiate faster chases. Every single one of these choices caused the match to risk falling apart purely as a result of the obsession doing everything they can to get in my way and reduce my stacks of Save the Best for Last. While there might be some other viable 4th perk choices, Play With Your Food is the one I’ve currently found to be the best at what I need a 4th perk slot to do.

FAQ
Q: Why did you do this/spend so much time on this?
A: When I first started writing this guide, I saw it as a side-hobby outside actually playing the game that I felt would help me understand Clown better in the process. I didn’t expect it to be more than 30-40 pages nor did I expect myself to eventually cover as many examples or topics. Throughout my time playing DBD as a Clown main, I’ve been approached by people while streaming who have asked me to make a guide so that they can learn to play like I do. As this happened more and more, I ended up dedicating more time and energy into making sure this guide did exactly that. There were plans for this guide to be even lengthier than it is currently, but I decided it would take too much time putting it off any longer.
Q: Why do you like Clown so much?
A: My love for Clown rose dramatically once I realized his yellow bottles were so powerful. It was almost like flipping a switch with how much it radically changed everything. Chases feel extremely easy and streamlined as Clown when compared to other M1 killers. I don’t find myself annoyed when I come up to certain pallets like I would if I were to play Sadako, Legion, Pig, etc. since Clown has the ability to hit survivors around most pre-dropped pallets.
Chases as Clown feel like solving a puzzle to me. Every pallet loop is a puzzle and hitting survivors is how you solve it. Clown’s bottles are the tools which make this happen, and I find it to be so satisfying. The only aspect of Clown I do not like is his poor map mobility, but you can’t give Clown better map mobility on account of how strong he is in chase. If Clown actually had good map mobility, he would be as overpowered as Blight or Nurse. You would have to nerf his strength in chase in order to make that happen and doing so would take away from what makes him so unique.
Q: Where/How did you learn about Clown’s yellow bottles and your technique?
A: Everything you see from how I play to what I have written in this guide is from my own experience. I started out not knowing how to use his yellow bottles at all until one day I started throwing them. As I started throwing them, I began identifying situations where they were strong and where they were weak. I then started throwing pinks after my yellows while I still had survivors at a pallet loop and realized that it was possible to combine both bottle effects with proper positioning. From there, I stopped kicking most pallets and began putting more emphasis on my movement. Randomly, one day, I decided to try Zanshin Tactics and realized how invaluable it was at helping me use Clown’s power even better.
If I listened to what many other Clown players had to say about Clown’s yellow bottles, there’s a very good chance I wouldn’t have learned any of these strategies. I have always had an innovative mind and I love thinking outside the box/re-inventing the wheel. I’ve learned what I have because I didn’t let other people discourage me from figuring out what they didn’t actually know for themselves.
Q: How strong do you think Clown is compared to other killers?
A: Overall? Middle of the pack. Like most killers, he is really great at one thing (chase) but really bad at the other (map mobility). This is what holds back most killers in the game from being as good as killers like Nurse or Blight who excel at both. I personally think that Clown is at least a top 5 killer in chase , but that’s irrelevant in the many situations where you can’t stop survivors from finishing generators.
Q: How would you buff Clown to make him better?
A: The hardest part about buffing Clown is doing it in a way that doesn’t make him too oppressive when he is already so powerful in chase. You can’t give him strong map mobility because then you’re just turning him into Nurse/Blight with bottles. You also can’t increase the potency of his bottles any further without possibly removing any and all forms of remaining counterplay against Clown in chase.
I feel like the best way to buff Clown without impacting the extent of how well he can perform is to look at his base bottle count and any add-ons that influence his utility. Right now, there’s hardly any reason to run any add-on in my tier list that’s below the A-tier category. Some of these add-ons could be buffed or reworked to have better effects. Kerosene Can , for example, could be buffed to last 60 seconds instead of 30 seconds. I would personally love to see Tattoo’s Middle Finger reworked to make yellow clouds activate instantly but only last for 3 seconds instead of 5. My go-to add-ons are almost always Sticky Soda Bottle / Cheap Gin Bottle along with either Cigar Box or Flask of Bleach . Anything other than those add-ons just isn’t as optimal.
If they increased Clown’s base bottle count to 5 bottles and reworked/buffed some of his weaker add-ons, it would put Clown in a better spot without enabling him to do anything more than he already could.
Q: Do you ever go for winstreaks on Clown? If so, what’s your highest winstreak?
A: I haven’t ever explicitly attempted a winstreak on Clown. To be very clear, I haven’t recorded or kept a record of how many matches I have won in a row. That being said, I can say with confidence in the past that my ‘theoretical’ winstreak is around 40. Before much of the meta and MMR changes over these past two years, I was able to play Clown for consecutive streams without losing a match. Even though I didn’t actually take note of how many matches this was, I base my possible streak at 40 around the average of how many matches I was playing per stream.
Nowadays, winstreaking on Clown at high MMR is just sadly impractical unless one is using his Pinkie Finger add-on. There are too many contingent survivor-sided factors that when combined together are unreasonably insurmountable. All it takes is for you to get sent to RPD or Lery’s against one survivor team with 4x meta loadouts, purple item + iri add-ons, and voice comms who effectively pre-run, split generators, bodyblock, and pre-drop uncounterable pallets for you to end the match with two kills at best. Anything more than that isn’t the result of you playing well, but the survivors playing poorly. If there was a casual mode without MMR, winstreaking might be worth attempting since more matches would be a mixed bag.
Q: Do you only play Clown, or do you also play other killers?
A: For the most part, I play almost entirely and only Clown. There was a time where I was playing Chucky on the side, but recently I haven’t played anyone else but Clown. While some people might find it to be repetitive to play only the same killer continuously, I derive interest from exploring the full extent of Clown’s power and also teaching other people how to do the same.
Q: I haven’t seen you use X perk or Y build in quite some time. Is it still good?
A: Just because I haven’t used something recently doesn’t mean it’s bad or that I don’t like it anymore. There’s only one of me, and I can only play one build per match. That build is limited to two add-ons and four perks. I have to decide with every match whether I want to play a build I like (of which I like many builds) or if I want to try something new . A lot of times, I’m testing new ideas and, thus, not using older or more familiar builds in turn.
If a perk or build is suggested within the guide, it’s very likely still viable even if some aspect of it has been nerfed in some fashion.

Q: How do you have so many Clown add-ons?
A: It’s due to multiple factors. First (and probably most important), I spend just about ALL of my bloodpoints on Clown. Other than what I spent prestiging the other killers so that I could unlock their perks, literally almost every bloodpoint I’ve earned over the last couple of years has been invested into Clown. I wouldn’t be surprised if I’ve spent over 300 million bloodpoints so far.
Second, I specifically take into account bloodpoint incentives and events with respect to deciding what offerings and add-ons I’m going to bring. During anniversaries, I typically don’t bring Cigar Boxes so that way I can stockpile a massive surplus from the yearly BP offerings. This surplus is usually enough to last me until the end of the year if you also include the Black Wards that come with it.
Lastly, speaking of Black Wards, a really big part of stockpiling your rare add-ons is knowing when to use them. When I’m running two purple rarity add-ons together outside of events (such as Cigar Box or Garish Makeup Kit), I’ll usually bring a Black Ward due to how it efficiently counts as two different purple add-ons. Finding specifically the same two purple add-ons you use every match isn’t that common for each bloodweb. You’re likely to get one add-on or the other. A single Black Ward in the bloodweb goes very far for that reason.
